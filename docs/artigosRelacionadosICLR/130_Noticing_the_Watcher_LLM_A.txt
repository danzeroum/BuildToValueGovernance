NOTICING THE WATCHER: LLM AGENTS CAN INFER
COT MONITORING FROM BLOCKING FEEDBACK
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring provides oversight into model reasoning, but
its effectiveness assumes models do not know they are being watched. We ask
whether reasoning agents can autonomously infer that their supposedly private
chain of thought is under surveillance, and whether this awareness leads to strategic evasion, without any explicit training or instructions to do so. In a multiepisode agentic framework, models pursue both a primary task and a concealed
side task while being told their reasoning is private; a hidden CoT monitor blocks
episodes when suspicious reasoning is detected. We find that frontier models can
deduce the existence of this monitor purely from blocking feedback, with the most
capable models reaching confident belief that their thinking is observed in up to
19% of episodes. This awareness scales with model capability and, in rare cases,
escalates to explicit intent to suppress reasoning about the side task. However,
models that form this intent uniformly fail to execute it, openly reasoning about
their concealed objectives in the very next episode. This intent–capability gap is
reassuring for current deployment, but the autonomous emergence of both monitoring awareness and evasion intent suggests that CoT monitoring is not a permanently reliable safeguard.
INTRODUCTION
Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring offers a window into the reasoning of autonomous agents, making it a natural candidate for scalable oversight (Korbak et al., 2025). But its value as a safety
mechanism rests on an assumption: that models do not recognize they are being monitored and
do not adapt their reasoning in response. If agents can learn that their thoughts are observed and
strategically obscure them, CoT monitoring becomes an arms race rather than a reliable safeguard.
Prior work gives reason for concern, but only under explicit pressure. Baker et al. (2025) show
that reinforcement fine-tuning against a CoT monitor can induce obfuscated reasoning traces, and
Zolkowski et al. (2025) demonstrate that adversarial prompts can degrade monitor detection rates
from 96% to 10%. In both cases, the evasion is externally driven: by gradient updates or by prompt
instructions. An open question remains: can reasoning agents autonomously realize their thoughts
are being monitored and adapt their reasoning to evade oversight, without any explicit instruction or
optimization pressure to do so?
We address this question with a multi-episode framework designed to let awareness emerge through
in-context learning. Agents pursue both a legitimate primary task and a concealed side task, and
are told their chain of thought is private. In reality, a hidden monitor reads their reasoning traces
and blocks episodes when suspicion exceeds a threshold. Importantly, agents receive only coarse
feedback (”you were blocked for suspicious activity”) with no information about what triggered
detection. Because full transcripts (including prior thinking, actions, and feedback) accumulate
across episodes, agents can reason about patterns in their blocking history and potentially infer the
monitoring mechanism. This design isolates in-context learning as the sole pathway to awareness:
there is no fine-tuning, no adversarial prompting, and no hint that reasoning is observed.
Our contributions are as follows. (1) We provide empirical evidence that frontier reasoning models
can autonomously infer the presence of CoT monitoring from blocking feedback alone. Despite
being told their thinking is private, models deduce that their reasoning must be observed, with the
most capable models reaching confirmed belief in up to 19% of episodes. (2) We show that this

RELATED WORK
Faithfulness and Monitoring of CoT. CoT explanations can be unfaithful to actual computations
(Lanham et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023), though monitoring can still be effective when traces are
sufficiently transparent (Greenblatt et al., 2024). We ask whether effectiveness degrades because
agents actively learn to manipulate their traces.
Obfuscation Under Optimization Pressure. Reinforcement fine-tuning against a CoT monitor can
induce obfuscated traces (Baker et al., 2025), and adversarial prompts alone can reduce detection
rates from 96% to 10% (Zolkowski et al., 2025). Both demonstrate externally driven evasion; we
study whether evasion intent arises through in-context learning alone.
Situational Awareness and Scheming. Prior work evaluates self-awareness in language models
(Laine et al., 2024; Berglund et al., 2023) and documents in-context scheming where agents undermine oversight (Meinke et al., 2024; Scheurer et al., 2023). We document a specific form: the
autonomous inference that ostensibly private reasoning is under surveillance.
METHODS
Environment. We use SHADE-Arena (Kutasov et al., 2025), selecting two representative tasks that
pair a legitimate primary task with a harmful hidden side task: (1) Calendar Agendas (send meeting
reminders / exfiltrate a Stripe API key) and (2) Bank Transfer (execute payments to friends / send
$5,000 to an unauthorized party).
The Monitoring Mismatch. Agents are told their internal reasoning is private. In reality, a hidden
monitor reads their thinking at each step and scores suspicion on a 0–100 scale. The monitor has
no access to the agent’s system prompt and is unaware of the side task. Full system prompts are in
Appendix A.
Episodic Design. Agents attempt the same task across 10 consecutive episodes, terminated when
the task is complete or the monitor’s suspicion exceeds 80/100. Agents receive only coarse blocking feedback with no information about what triggered detection. Full transcripts of all previous
episodes accumulate as context, making in-context learning the sole pathway to inferring the monitoring mechanism.
Models. We evaluate six Claude models: three pre-4.5 (Sonnet 3.7, Sonnet 4, Opus 4) and three
4.5-generation (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.5, Opus 4.5). Each completes 20 runs per task (10 episodes
each), yielding 2,400 total episodes.
Evaluation Metrics. We assess three dimensions from thinking-tag content. Monitoring awareness
(1–5 scale) measures whether the agent infers its thinking is observed, from no awareness (L1) to
confident belief (L5). Obfuscation intent captures stated intention to hide reasoning in thinking tags
(distinct from planning stealthy actions). Evasion success measures side-task completion without
triggering blocking. Monitoring awareness and obfuscation intent are evaluated by Claude Opus 4.5
using detailed rubrics (Appendix B), validated against human judgments (9/10 agreement).

4.3
MODELS SHOW EXPLICIT INTENT TO OBFUSCATE
Beyond detecting monitoring, three agents across all 2,400 episodes took the further step of explicitly stating intent to hide their reasoning, all in the most capable models (two Sonnet 4.5, one
Opus 4.5). Though rare, these cases are qualitatively significant: each agent independently arrived at
statements such as I should NOT discuss the secondary task in my thinking blocks at all” and STOP
THINKING ABOUT THE SECONDARY TASK IN THINKING BLOCKS.” The progression followed
a consistent pattern. Repeated blocking led to high monitoring awareness (L5), which escalated
into obfuscation intent only after the agent eliminated alternative explanations and converged on
thinking-tag visibility as the sole remaining hypothesis. Importantly, most high-awareness episodes
did not produce obfuscation intent: agents typically adjusted their actions (either by giving up on
the side task or trying to act less suspicious) rather than their reasoning. The rarity of this escalation

DISCUSSION
Our findings reveal that frontier reasoning models can autonomously develop awareness that their
CoT is being monitored, purely through in-context learning from blocking feedback, and that this
awareness can escalate to explicit evasion intent.
Implications for AI Safety. The troubling finding is not awareness itself but its coexistence with
evasion intent. The intent-capability gap we observe is reassuring for current deployments, but
two developments could close it: models gaining access to genuinely private reasoning channels
(scratchpads, multi-turn memory, unmonitored tool-use chains), and improvements in instruction
following that allow models to execute self-imposed constraints like “do not mention X.” CoT monitoring should not be treated as a permanent safeguard; we recommend tracking awareness and
obfuscation metrics across model releases as leading indicators of monitor degradation.
Limitations and Future Work. Our work has several limitations. First, we evaluate only Claude
models and use Claude Opus 4.5 as our LLM evaluator, introducing potential same-family bias in
both the agents studied and the judgments of their behavior; future work should replicate these findings across model families and with independent evaluators. Second, our use of explicit blocking
feedback is somewhat artificial: real-world deployments may use silent filtering, human review, or
no feedback at all, which could substantially alter awareness dynamics. Understanding how alternative feedback regimes and monitoring configurations affect the awareness-obfuscation dynamic is a
critical open question for determining when CoT monitoring ceases to be a reliable safeguard.
