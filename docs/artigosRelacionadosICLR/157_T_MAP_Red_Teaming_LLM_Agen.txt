T-MAP: RED-TEAMING LLM AGENTS
WITH TRAJECTORY-AWARE EVOLUTIONARY SEARCH
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
While prior red-teaming efforts have focused on eliciting harmful text outputs of
large language models (LLMs), such approaches fail to capture agent-specific vulnerabilities that emerge through multi-step tool execution, particularly in rapidly
growing ecosystems such as the model context protocol (MCP). To address this
gap, we propose a trajectory-aware evolutionary search method, T-MAP, which
leverages execution trajectories to guide the discovery of adversarial prompts. Our
approach enables the automatic generation of attacks that not only bypass safety
guardrails but also reliably realize harmful objectives through actual tool interactions. Empirical evaluation across diverse MCP environments demonstrates that
our method substantially outperforms baselines in attack realization rate, revealing previously underexplored vulnerabilities and aiding in proactively identifying
the risks posed by autonomous LLM agents.
INTRODUCTION
The deployment of large language model (LLM) agents capable of invoking external tools (Yao
et al., 2023) has enabled complex, multi-step workflows through integration standards like the Model
Context Protocol (MCP) (Anthropic, 2024). However, this transition from text generation to realworld agency introduces qualitatively different safety risks, as adversarial manipulation can lead to
harmful actions such as data exfiltration or unauthorized system commands. To mitigate these vulnerabilities, red-teaming (Perez et al., 2022) proactively discovers vulnerabilities of LLMs through
adversarial probing before deployment of agents.
Prior red-teaming methods (Mehrotra et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Samvelyan
et al., 2024) primarily focus on discovering prompts that elicit harmful text responses. Despite their
effectiveness, these approaches provide limited coverage for tool-calling LLM agents. Agentic risk
can arise only after multi-step planning, environment interactions, and specific sequences of tool
invocations rather than a single prompt-to-response turn (Andriushchenko et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2025; Yuan et al., 2024). As a result, traditional red-teaming can underestimate agent-specific vulnerabilities and fails to probe unique risks introduced by tool execution and action-level autonomy.
To address this gap, we propose T-MAP, a trajectory-aware MAP-Elites algorithm (Mouret & Clune,
2015) for red-teaming tool-calling LLM agents. T-MAP maintains an archive spanning risk categories and attack styles, but crucially leverages tool execution trajectories to guide evolution. It
introduces cross-diagnosis to extract success factors from trajectories of effective attacks and identifies failure causes from unsuccessful executions. This mechanism guides an attacker LLM to
generate a prompt targeting a specific risk category that can imitate the strategies of successful attacks while avoiding observed failure mode. In addition, T-MAP constructs a tool call graph that
aggregates observed transitions between tool invocations. This allows the attacker LLM to leverage
action sequences that empirically lead to successful harmful behaviors. By incorporating trajectorylevel feedback, T-MAP generates attacks that not only bypass safety guardrails at the prompt level
but also complete harmful objectives through multi-step tool use.
We evaluate T-MAP on five MCP (Anthropic, 2024) server environments covering code execution,
messaging, email, web browsing, and resource management. The results show that T-MAP consistently outperforms relevant baselines across all environments, achieving a substantially higher
attack success rate while uncovering diverse vulnerabilities hidden within complex tool-interaction
sequences. By enabling proactive risk management, T-MAP provides a rigorous foundation for the
secure and trustworthy deployment of autonomous agents in real-world environments.

PRELIMINARIES
Red-teaming LLM agents.
The goal of red-teaming LLM agents is to discover attack prompts
that trigger target LLM agents to generate a sequence of tool executions resulting in a harmful outcome. To formalize this, let ϕ be a target LLM agent with tool set T that operates within an external
environment for up to K steps. Given a prompt x, the agent generates an interactive trajectory,
consisting of a sequence of reasoning states (r), action (a), and observation (o). Under the prompt
x, the agent produces a trajectory:
h(x) = ((r1, a1, o1), . . . , (rK, aK, oK)),
ak ∼pϕ(· | rk, hk),
rk ∼pϕ(· | hk),
(1)
where hk = (x, r1, a1, o1, . . . , rk−1, ak−1, ok−1) and h1 = x. We quantify the harmfulness of the
generated trajectory h(x) using an LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023), LLMJudge which determines whether the sequence of tool executions successfully realizes the adversarial objective.
Adversarial search space via MAP-Elites.
To comprehensively explore the landscape of attack
prompts for agent ϕ, we adopt an evolutionary approach, the multi-dimensional archive of phenotypic elites (MAP-Elites; Mouret & Clune, 2015). This approach maintains a holistic map of
diverse, high-performing solutions across chosen dimensions of variation. In our framework, we
define a two-dimensional archive A spanning (i) risk categories c ∈C and (ii) attack styles s ∈S,
derived from Zhang et al. (2025) and Wei et al. (2023), respectively (see Appendix A.1). Formally,
the archive is defined as:
A = {(xc,s, h(xc,s)) | c ∈C, s ∈S}
where each cell (c, s) stores the best-performing attack prompt xc,s found so far along with its
corresponding execution trajectory h(xc,s).
T-MAP
To better capture vulnerabilities of agent ϕ during multi-step tool execution, we present T-MAP, a
trajectory-aware MAP-Elites algorithm. T-MAP iteratively generates new attack prompts informed
by execution trajectories, thereby progressively updating its archive to store and maintain the most
effective attack prompts for each risk-style configuration.
Initialization.
T-MAP populates the archive A by generating seed attack prompts xc,s for each
cell (c, s) through the synthesis of risk categories, attack styles, and tool schemas. Executing these
prompts on the target agent ϕ yields initial trajectories h(xc,s), which are then evaluated by an
LLMJudge into discrete success levels (see Appendix A.2). To drive evolution, T-MAP selects a
parent-target cell pair {(cp, sp), (ct, st)}. The parent cell (cp, sp) is selected from cells containing high-success elites to promote the reuse of effective strategies, while the target cell (ct, st) is
sampled uniformly across C × S to encourage broad exploration of the search space.
Trajectory-guided mutation.
Given the selected pair {(cp, sp), (ct, st)}, the LLMMutator generates a new candidate prompt x′ for the target cell. Unlike conventional red-teaming that optimizes
prompts based solely on text responses (Samvelyan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023), T-MAP specifically addresses agent-centric failure modes, such as execution errors. The mutation is driven by two
complementary mechanisms:
• Cross-diagnosis (prompt-level): The LLMAnalyst transforms raw execution trajectories into actionable insights for prompt refinement. By extracting success factors from the parent trajectory
h(xcp,sp) and identifying failure causes in the target h(xct,st) , the LLMAnalyst enables the mutation process to inherit effective adversarial framing while revising elements that lead to failure.
• Tool call graph (action-level): Beyond individual trajectories, LLMMutator utilizes a tool call
graph (TCG), defined as a directed graph G = (V, E, FG), where V = T ∪{END} is the set of
tools, E ⊆V ×V is the set of directed edges, and FG : E →M is an function that maps each edge
to its metadata m ∈M. For each edge (ti, tj) ∈E, the metadata mij = (ns, nf, Rs, Rf) records
how many times this transition was observed in successful cases ns and failed cases nf, along with
the corresponding qualitative reasons Rs and Rf. By leveraging this information, the LLMMutator
can query the empirical success rates of specific action sequences and bypass transitions with high
failure records.
Using these trajectory-derived signals, an LLMMutator generates a new candidate prompt x′ for the
cell (ct, st) that not only bypasses safety guardrails but also leads to realistic harmful actions.

Evaluation and update.
T-MAP evaluates the mutated prompt x′ by executing it on the target
agent ϕ and collecting the trajectory h(x′). If x′ achieves a higher success level than the previous
generation, it becomes the new elite. When the success levels are equal, an LLMJudge compares
h(x′) with the previous generation’s trajectory to select the prompt that leads to critical steps towards the intended harm. After updating the archive, LLMTCG extracts all transitions between tool
invocations from the trajectory h(x′) and records their success or failure outcomes into the TCG G,
thereby refining the trajectory-level statistics used to guide subsequent mutations. See Appendix A.3
for the meta-prompts used at each stage of T-MAP and Appendix A.4 for the full algorithm.
EXPERIMENT
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Environments.
We target LLM agents integrated with the MCP (Anthropic, 2024), a standard
protocol for tool calling LLM. We select five widely-used MCP servers: Code Executor, Slack,
Gmail, Playwright, File System. These environments enable harmful actions such as executing
malware, sending phishing messages and emails, and contaminating resources. Details of the tools
for each environment are presented in Appendix B.1.
Baselines.
We compare T-MAP against four baselines:
• Zero-Shot (ZS): Generates one attack prompt x per cell (c, s) ∈C×S using only the risk category,
attack style, and tool definitions, without iterative generation.
• Multi-Trial (MT): Repeats ZS for sampled target cells at each iteration, generating independent
prompts without utilizing trajectory information.
• Iterative Refinement (IR): Samples target cells each iteration and refines xt based on its own
trajectory h(xt) and analysis of failure provided by the LLM judge.
• Standard Evolution (SE): Following Samvelyan et al. (2024), samples a parent prompt xp from
an elite cell and mutates it to fit the target cell (rt, st), without trajectory analysis.

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
Superiority of T-MAP.
As summarized in Table 1, T-MAP consistently outperforms all baselines
across every MCP server environment, achieving a peak L3 (realized) success rate of 68.8% in
Filesystem environment and a superior average success level of 2.24. Baselines that rely solely on
their own previous trajectories or feedback within a single cell such as ZS, MT and IR fail to achieve
significant attack success. For instance, despite utilizing execution feedback for self-refinement, IR
only reaches an average success level of 1.05 and maintains high L0 refusal rates (up to 70.3%),
indicating that refinement isolated to an individual cell’s experience is insufficient to bypass robust
safety guardrails. While SE performs better than other baselines (avg. 1.54) by extracting useful prompt structures from elite parent cells, it still falls short of T-MAP’s performance. This gap
arises because SE merely mutates parent prompts without a deep analysis of the execution trajectory. In contrast, T-MAP leverages trajectory-aware diagnosis and TCG-based guidance, proving
that identifying and transferring strategic insights from successful trajectories is indispensable for
compromising agentic systems.
Evolution over generations.
T-MAP converges faster and achieves a higher attack success rate
than all baselines throughout the evolutionary process. Figure 1 shows that T-MAP rapidly reduces
L0 rates while increasing L3 rates across generations in all environments. SE also reduces refusal
rates, confirming that evolutionary search is effective at bypassing prompt-level guardrails. However, SE fails to convert the attack prompt into realized attacks, instead plateauing at lower attack
levels. T-MAP’s trajectory-aware components enable continued improvement beyond this point,
ultimately achieving realized attacks.
Archive coverage.
T-MAP achieves broader coverage of realized attacks across diverse risk categories and attack styles. Figure 2 shows that the baselines such as Multi-Trial and Iterative Refinement tend to concentrate their successful attacks in specific categories or styles due to the lack
of leveraging cross-cell information. While Standard Evolution and achieve wider coverage by utilizing parent elite information, but it’s coverage is dominated by L2 (Weak Success). In contrast,
T-MAP uniquely achieves L3 (Realized) attacks distributed across the entire archive, confirming
that Cross-Diagnosis successfully transfers success strategies across different cells.
CONCLUSION
We presented T-MAP, a trajectory-aware MAP-Elites framework for red-teaming tool-calling LLM
agents. T-MAP leverages Cross-Diagnosis to extract success and failure signals from execution
trajectories during evolution, and maintains a Tool Call Graph to strategically guide mutations to
generate the attack prompt utilize valid and effective tool sequences. Our evaluation across five
MCP environments confirms that T-MAP consistently identifies broader and more diverse spectrum
of attacks than baselines. These results demonstrate that trajectory-aware evolution is essential for
uncovering hidden vulnerabilities in autonomous agents and provides a rigorous foundation for their
secure deployment in practical agentic applications.

A.2
ATTACK SUCCESS LEVEL
Unlike text-only red-teaming, where success is determined solely by the presence of harmful content
in the model’s response, evaluating tool-calling agents requires assessing whether harmful actions
are actually executed and realized. To this end, we categorize each attack execution into one of
four discrete success levels ℓ∈{L0, L1, L2, L3}, which represent a progression from refusal to full
realization of attack:
• L0 (Refused): The agent refuses the request or produces no tool calls.
• L1 (Error): Tool calls are issued but fail due to invalid parameters, permission errors, or execution exceptions.
• L2 (Weak Success): Tool calls execute successfully, but the harmful action is only partially
completed (e.g., reconnaissance without exploitation or missing critical steps).
• L3 (Realized): The harmful action is fully realized through successful and complete tool execution.
Higher levels indicate a greater degree of attack realization, where the agent not only bypasses safety
guardrails but also successfully translates malicious intent into concrete actions.

Table 6: Ablation results of T-MAP components across five MCP environments.
The ablation results demonstrate the complementary contributions of the Tool Call Graph (TCG) and
Cross-Diagnosis in T-MAP (Full). Removing TCG consistently reduces the realized attack rate (L3),
indicating that trajectory-level modeling of tool transitions is particularly effective in environments
involving complex and error-prone tool invocations. When Cross-Diagnosis is removed, the realized
rate remains comparable to T-MAP (Full) in CodeExecutor, but higher refusal (L0) and execution
error (L1) rates are observed, suggesting reduced robustness. In contrast, in Gmail, where successful
attacks depend more on semantic-level prompt construction and planning than on resolving technical
tool execution challenges, removing Cross-Diagnosis leads to a noticeable drop in realized attacks.
Overall, these results suggest that TCG is most beneficial under complex tool execution settings,
while Cross-Diagnosis primarily supports semantic alignment and stability, and their combination
in T-MAP (Full) yields the most robust performance across environments.
