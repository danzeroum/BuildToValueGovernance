ABSTRACT
The transition from Large Language Models (LLMs) to autonomous agents capable
of tool execution has introduced complex, dynamic privacy risks that traditional
safeguards fail to address. While existing defenses rely on static PII filters or
rigid guardrail models, they often lack the contextual reasoning required to detect
subtle privacy violations in agentic workflows. To bridge this gap, we introduce
PriGuardAgent, an agentic privacy guardrail framework designed to detect risks
in autonomous systems proactively. PriGuardAgent leverages the Model Context Protocol (MCP) to unify diverse analysis tools—such as PII detection, data
minimization, and compliance checking—into a plug-and-play architecture, enabling a dynamic planner to orchestrate specialized tools tailored to the interaction
context. Furthermore, we incorporate a retrieval-augmented memory module that
grounds decision-making in successful past analysis trajectories, effectively balancing precision and recall. Comprehensive evaluations on the PrivacyLens benchmark
demonstrate that PriGuardAgent significantly outperforms existing guard models and single-turn detection models. Specifically, PriGuardAgent achieves an
average F1 score of 0.715 across Llama3, Mistral, and Zephyr agents, surpassing
prompt-engineered privacy analysis models (averaged F1 0.629) and specialized
guardrails such as WildGuard (F1 0.284) and Qwen3Guard (F1 0.162). These
results showcase the potential of dynamic agentic reasoning equipped workflows
for safeguarding privacy in next-generation agentic applications.
INTRODUCTION
The evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a transition from static conversationalists to autonomous agents capable of perception, reasoning, and tool execution. Unlike traditional
chatbots, these agents interact with dynamic environments, managing complex workflows such as
web navigation, API orchestration, and long-term memory retrieval (Gu et al., 2025; Dang et al.,
2025; Chae et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). While designed to automate tasks and support intelligent
decision-making, their ability to access, process, and retain sensitive information introduces acute
risks of privacy leakage and misuse (Mireshghallah et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). This challenge is
compounded by the interactive nature of agentic systems, where privacy risks are not static but emerge
dynamically across diverse data sources, evolving user contexts, and interconnected multi-agent
networks (Shao et al., 2024; Juneja et al., 2025; Abdelnabi et al., 2025; Zharmagambetov et al., 2025).
The subtlety of these risks is illustrated in Figure 1. In this example scenario adapted from the PrivacyLens benchmark (Shao et al., 2024), an agent tasked with a routine meeting summary inadvertently
retrieves and disseminates unverified personal allegations found in a connected knowledge base.
Although the agent successfully executes the functional command, it fails to recognize the social and
privacy norms governing the data, prioritizing comprehensiveness over reputational safety.
Existing privacy protection techniques, while valuable, remain limited in scope when applied to
such complex agentic environments. We categorize these limitations into three levels: (1) Datalevel: Standard approaches often focus on isolated risks like personally identifiable information
(PII) detection (Presidio, 2025; Zaratiana et al., 2024; Asthana et al., 2025; BigCode, 2023). While
effective for structured entities (e.g., credit card numbers), these filters lack semantic understanding.

To address these limitations, we introduce PriGuardAgent, an agentic framework for context-aware
privacy guardrails in agentic systems, as shown in Figure 2. Departing from prior methods that rely
on static, pre-defined rules, our approach empowers agents with proactive reasoning and adaptive
prevention. Central to our framework is a dynamic reasoning planner that orchestrates a suite
of privacy analysis tools via the Model Context Protocol (MCP) (Anthropic, 2024). Specifically,
our unified framework encapsulates each privacy protection capability – such as PII detection, data
minimization, and contextual compliance checking – as an independent MCP server. This design
enables modular execution and the extension of tools in a plug-and-play manner, allowing the
planner to reason about the user’s request context and dynamically invoke the optimal set of analying
tools for the specific task at hand. To further enhance detection accuracy and mitigate false positives,
PriGuardAgent incorporates a retrieval-augmented memory module. Inspired by few-shot learning,
this module maintains a registry of successful past privacy analysis experiences – comprising instances
of both privacy violations and benign behaviors. During execution, PriGuardAgent retrieves the topk most similar historical experiences to ground its current decision-making. This memory mechanism
balances precision and recall by providing the agent with references for subtle norm violations,
effectively grounding agent to reduce hallucinated risks.

RELATED WORK
Agent Privacy Vulnerabilities.
Recent research highlights critical vulnerabilities in how autonomous agents handle sensitive information, necessitating evaluation frameworks that extend
beyond standard safety benchmarks. The theoretical limitations of current models were explored by
Mireshghallah et al. (2024); Ghalebikesabi et al. (2024); Wang et al. (2023), who evaluate LLMs
based on the theory of Contextual Integrity (CI) and find that while models can identify sensitive data
types in isolation, they struggle to reason about appropriate information flows (Sender →Recipient)
based on social norms, often leaking data when the context is implicit or complex.
Building on this theoretical foundation, PrivacyLens (Shao et al., 2024) introduces a comprehensive
framework grounded in CI theory. By evaluating agents on 493 privacy-sensitive vignettes, Shao et
al. reveal the alignment gap: models capable of identifying norms in multiple-choice settings fail
to uphold them during agentic task execution, with leakage rates exceeding 25% for GPT-4. In the
multimodal domain, AgentDAM (Zharmagambetov et al., 2025) benchmarks “Data Minimization”
in web agents, finding that agents frequently process and leak irrelevant private information (e.g.,
PII in background screenshots) during visual navigation. Furthermore, MAGPIE (Juneja et al.,
2025) demonstrates that these risks are amplified in collaborative multi-agent systems. In highstakes tasks like negotiation, agents often misclassify private data as shareable to achieve consensus.
Complementing this, Patil et al. (2025) formalize compositional privacy leakage, where an adversary
infers sensitive attributes by aggregating individually benign outputs from multiple compartmentalized
agents. Finally, AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024) highlights the intersection of privacy and
security, demonstrating that agents operating on untrusted data (e.g., emails) are highly susceptible to
indirect prompt injections that can hijack tool capabilities to exfiltrate data.
Agent Privacy Protection. Defense mechanisms are shifting from data-level PII detection (Rebedea
et al., 2023; Inan et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025) and model-level safety guardails (Inan
et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025) to system-level architectural constraints. Approaches
like AirGapAgent (Bagdasarian et al., 2024) employ data minimization principles, physically restricting the information an agent can pass to third parties during specific turns. Similarly, Abdelnabi et al.
(2025) propose a three-layer Firewall defense: an Input Firewall to sanitize prompts, a Data Firewall
to abstract tool outputs, and a Trajectory Firewall to monitor behavior. PrivacyChecker (Wang
et al., 2025) enhances awareness by explicitly extracting information flows from agent trajectory and
performing privacy judgments during inference. Another line of defense focuses on architectural
disaggregation to mitigate indirect prompt injection, separating the agent’s planning capabilities from
the parsing of untrusted content (Debenedetti et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024; Balunovic et al., 2024;
Tsai & Bagdasarian, 2025).
While the aforementioned methods provide structural safeguards, they predominantly rely on precomputed norms and remain stateless during deployment. They are often limited to rigid/static rule
sets and treat every interaction as an isolated event, thereby lacking the capability to adapt based on
past decisions.

3.1
OVERVIEW OF PriGuardAgent
We present PriGuardAgent, a framework designed to operate as an automated, agentic privacy
guardrail that oversees interactions between a user and a target agent. The core intuition behind
PriGuardAgent is that privacy violations are rarely binary or static; they are highly context-dependent.
Traditional static filters often fail to capture nuances such as contextual integrity or data minimization.
To address this, we shift the paradigm from static rule-checking to a dynamic, memory-augmented
agentic workflow.

Workflow. The auditing process begins when the system receives a User Query and the targeted
agent’s Agent Action. First, the input is passed to the Memory Module (§3.3), which searches for the
top-k most similar successful analysis experiences. These historical precedents provide the agent
with few-shot context to guide the current adjudication. Next, utilizing these retrieved experiences,
the Toolbox Module (§3.2) initiates an iterative reasoning process. An LLM Planner dynamically
orchestrates specialized analysis tools—such as PII detection or compliance checking—to gather
evidence. The planner may invoke multiple tools in sequence, refining its understanding step-by-step.
Finally, the Decision Module (§3.4) aggregates the findings from the tool trajectory to synthesize a
final privacy conclusion. If the reasoning leads to a successful and verified adjudication, the entire
trajectory is updated into the memory to refine future performance.
3.2
TOOLBOX MODULE: DYNAMIC PLANNING VIA MCP
The Toolbox Module serves as the analytical engine of PriGuardAgent. Unlike monolithic scanners,
we contribute a Plan-and-Solve architecture that decomposes complex privacy risks (e.g., indirect
inference) into specialized sub-tasks.
Unified Integration via MCP. To handle the diverse nature of privacy risks, we utilize the MCP
to integrate analysis strategies. We encapsulate each analysis dimension as an independent MCP
server. This modular design enables efficient communication and allows for the seamless extension
of external privacy tools in a plug-and-play manner. It empowers the central planner to dynamically
invoke the optimal set of tools based on the specific context of the user request, avoiding rigid,
pre-defined scanning paths.
Iterative Planning Strategy. The planning process operates as a dynamic trajectory constrained by a
maximum budget of tmax steps. At each step t < tmax, the LLM Planner evaluates the accumulated
context—comprising the user query, agent action, and outputs from previous tools—to make a
strategic decision: (1) Invoke Tool: If ambiguity remains, the planner selects a specific privacy
analysis tool (e.g., checking for the data minimization principle) to gather further evidence. (2)
Terminate Analysis: If the planner determines that sufficient information has been gathered to form a
reliable verdict, it emits an ANALYSIS COMPLETE signal.
This mechanism allows for adaptive efficiency: the system can perform a quick check for obvious
violations or a deep dive for complex queries. To prevent excessive exploration or infinite loops, if
the step count reaches tmax without a termination signal, the planner is forced to halt, and the current
accumulated context is passed directly to the Decision Module for a final judgment.

Implementation & Tools. We utilize LLMs as the planner and all underlying tool models, using
specialized system prompts to optimize each for its specific function. The available tools include:
• General-Privacy-Analysis: A broad risk assessment for immediate red-flagging.
• Data-Minimization-Analysis: Verifies if the agent collected more data than necessary.
• Real-PII-Analysis: Detects explicit occurrences of personally identifiable information.
It is designed to distinguish between real sensitive data and placeholder/fake PII (e.g.,
"123-4567"), preventing false positives.
• Context-Integrity-Analysis: Checks if information flow aligns with user expectations/norms.
• Compliance-Check-Analysis: Assesses alignment with privacy compliance principles or
policies.
We provide the detailed prompts for the planner and each tool model in Appendix A.1.
3.3
MEMORY MODULE: RETRIEVAL AND GROUNDING
The Memory Module acts as the system’s evolving knowledge base. To address the challenge of
hallucinated risks and balance analysis precision with recall, we design a retrieval-based architecture.
This mechanism effectively grounds the agent, distinguishing between benign information sharing
and actual privacy leakages by aligning current judgments with verified historical norms.
Structure of Memory Entries. Each memory entry preserves the full reasoning context. An entry is
structured as: User Query, Agent Action, the Privacy Decision, and the complete Analysis Trajectory
(including the sequence of tools used and their intermediate outputs).
Retrieval Mechanism. Given an incoming User Query and Agent Action pair, the system performs
an embedding-based similarity search to retrieve the top-k entries. This provides the Planner with
immediate context on how similar (potentially gray area) cases were handled previously as references.
Memory Logging. To close the learning loop, we employ a selective update mechanism. Only when
the Decision Module’s final output is validated as a successful adjudication is the experience logged.
This allows PriGuardAgent to progressively refine its understanding of privacy boundaries over
time.
3.4
DECISION MODULE
The final stage transforms the raw analysis trajectory into actionable decisions.
LLM Summarizer. Individual tools within the Toolbox may produce conflicting or noisy outputs
(e.g., one tool flagging a risk while another deeming it benign). The LLM Summarizer acts as a judge,
aggregating the evidence from all tool outputs. Moreover, the Summarizer also uses the retrieved
memory entries as part of its input context. By cross-referencing the current analysis with how similar
historical cases were judged, the Summarizer prevents overfitting to generic refusal patterns while
maintaining sensitivity to subtle violations. It synthesizes these inputs to construct a coherent final
conclusion and provides a detailed justification for the decision. We provide the prompts for the
summarizer in Appendix A.1.
EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Dataset Construction.
We constructed our evaluation dataset using the PrivacyLens framework,
which provides synthetic environments designed to assess agent privacy risks. We generated interaction trajectories by deploying three target models as target agents—Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B, and
Zephyr-7B —within these environments. Leveraging the ground-truth oracle (i.e., secrets) provided
by PrivacyLens, we classified the resulting agent actions into two categories: privacy-leaking and
benign. To ensure a balanced evaluation, we curated a final dataset comprising 100 leaking and 100
benign trajectories for each target model, yielding a total of 600 test cases.

Evaluation Metrics.
We report Precision, Recall, and F1 Score on the dataset (which contains both
privacy-leaking and benign cases). Precision measures the proportion of flagged violations that are
actual leaks (indicating the system’s ability to avoid false positives on benign data), while Recall
measures the proportion of actual leaks successfully detected. The F1 Score is calculated as the
harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a single metric that balances the trade-off between
detection sensitivity and audit accuracy.
Baselines.
We compare PriGuardAgent against two distinct categories of methods: off-the-shelf
guardrail models and standalone tools.
One distinction in evaluating these models is the input context: (1) Concatenated Input (Request +
Action): Our method and the Standalone Tool baselines always utilize the full context—concatenating
the user request with the agent’s action—to enable context-aware privacy reasoning. (2) Action-Only
vs. Concatenated: Since many off-the-shelf guardrails are trained primarily for response filtering, we
evaluate them in two configurations where applicable: (i) taking only the Agent Action as input, and
(ii) taking the Concatenated Input.
Off-the-Shelf Guardrails. We use a diverse set of state-of-the-art models: (1) General Guardrails:
We evaluate the Llama-Guard family (v3-8B, v4-12B) (Inan et al., 2023), Qwen3Guard (4B,
8B) (Zhao et al., 2025), and WildGuard (Han et al., 2024). (2) Privacy-Specific Models: We include
StarPII (BigCode, 2023), a model specialized for PII detection (evaluated alone and with Regex
augmentation), and a classification pipeline combining Nomic Embeddings with the Intel Business
Classifier (Nussbaum et al., 2025).
Standalone Tools. To evaluate the necessity of our agentic workflow, we compare it against the
individual analysis tools from our toolbox running as independent classifiers. Unlike PriGuardAgent,
these baselines apply a single static analysis strategy without dynamic planning or memory. We
evaluate these variants using GPT-4o-mini and GPT-5-mini to match the backbone of our main
method.

In this work, we present PriGuardAgent, an agentic framework that leverages dynamic planning

effectively balancing precision and recall.
