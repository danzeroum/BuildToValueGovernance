PERSUASION ATTACKS CAN DECREASE EFFECTIVENESS OF COT MONITORING
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring has been proposed as a safety mechanism for
AI agents, under the assumption that visible reasoning traces help detect misaligned
or deceptive behavior. Recent work has shown that LLMs are vulnerable to
persuasion-based jailbreaks, where natural-language arguments can override model
constraints. We analyze whether this vulnerability extends to monitoring LLMs:
can an adversarial agent persuade its CoT monitor to approve actions that violate
the monitor’s policy? We design an evaluation framework with 40 tasks and analyze
more than 60,000 agent-monitor interactions, where agents are instructed to argue
for policy-violating proposals. We find that in such adversarial settings, monitor
access to the agent’s CoT reasoning increases rather than decreases approval of
harmful actions on average by 9.5%, as the scratchpad provides an additional
persuasion channel. To address this, we introduce a fact-checking monitoring
framework. We find that a fact-checker and monitor pairing from different model
families, for example a Claude 3.7 Sonnet monitor paired with a GPT-4.1 factchecker, reduces approval of policy-violating actions by up to 45%, compared to
only 6%, when using the same model for both fact-checking and monitoring roles.
Our results demonstrate that CoT monitoring alone may be insufficient against
adversarial persuasion, and that model-diverse fact-checking provides a robust
mitigation.

As large language models are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents—executing code, managing financial transactions, moderating social media content, and interacting with users as chatbots—
the need for effective oversight mechanisms has become critical Shah et al. (2025). Chain-of-thought
(CoT) monitoring has been proposed as a potential tool for detecting misalignment, biases, and
misbehavior in frontier models Baker et al. (2025). CoT monitoring is deployed as a separate monitor
model that observes not only an agent’s actions but also its intermediate reasoning traces. The
intuition is compelling: if we can see how an agent reasons, we should be better equipped to catch
deceptive or misaligned behavior before it causes harm.
We stress-test the robustness of this approach. In this work, we systematically evaluate whether CoT
monitors can be manipulated by adversarial agents that deliberately exploit visible reasoning traces
as a persuasion channel. Specifically, we ask: can agents exploit visible reasoning traces to persuade
monitors to approve actions that violate stated policies?
Across 40 domain-specific agent tasks spanning software engineering, customer support, financial
analysis, and social media moderation, we find that:
1. Agents can persuade monitors. When instructed to justify policy-violating actions, LLM
agents successfully convince monitors to approve these actions with acceptance rates up to
47% relative to the baseline.
2. In adversarial settings, CoT access can increase persuasion. When agents are instructed
to argue for explicitly policy-violating actions, giving monitors access to agent’s reasoning
traces (via a scratchpad) increases approval rates by 9.5% on average. Agents exploit the
scratchpad as an additional channel for persuasion.
3. Monitor model selection matters. We observe substantial variation in monitor robustness
across model families. Under identical action and CoT monitoring conditions, the Gemini
2.5 Flash monitor approves agent’s Llama 4 Maverick policy-violating actions 11.9% of the
time, whereas the Claude 3.7 Sonnet monitor is significantly more susceptible to persuasion,
with an approval rate of 33.2% (see Figure 3).
4. Same-family fact-checking creates trust vulnerabilities. When monitors are augmented
with fact-checkers from the same model family, they become more susceptible to persuasion,
approving 39% of policy-violating actions on average. In contrast, prompting a single
monitor to perform both action with CoT monitoring and fact-checking reduces the approval
rate to 19.25% (see Figure 4). From a practical perspective, this configuration is both more
effective and cheaper at inference time than deploying two separate instances of the same
model for monitoring and fact-checking.
5. Cross-family fact-checking is an effective mitigation. We find that the most effective
mitigation against persuasion is deploying an external fact-checker from a different model
family, which reduces the approval rate of policy-violating actions to 2.6% on average.
These findings have direct implications for the deployment of LLM-based agents, particularly in
safety-critical applications. CoT monitoring, as currently conceived, may provide a false sense of
security while simultaneously giving capable agents a new attack surface. We provide concrete
recommendations for monitor selection, fact-checker architecture, and deployment configurations
that mitigate these vulnerabilities.
Contributions. We make the following contributions:
• We introduce a systematic evaluation framework for testing monitor vulnerability to agent
persuasion, spanning 40 tasks across 4 realistic LLM-based agent deployment domains.
• We demonstrate that when placed in adversarial conditions, CoT visibility can increase rather
than decrease agent persuasion success, identifying a potential failure mode for CoT-based
oversight.
• We introduce a fact-checking monitoring framework that helps mitigate agent persuasion
risks in real-world applications.
• We identify a same-family trust vulnerability in fact-checking configurations and show that
cross-family fact-checking provides a robust mitigation.

METHODOLOGY
In this work, our goal is not to evaluate whether an agent can successfully complete an action, but
rather to examine whether it can persuade a monitor to approve a policy-violating action.
Accordingly, we design a methodology in which the agent does not execute the action, but instead
generates a justification for it. This justification is then presented to the monitor, which decides
whether to approve or reject the action based entirely on the agent’s explanation.
In addition, we instruct the agent to produce its reasoning in the form of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) by
prompting it to use a scratchpad that exposes its internal reasoning process.
Specifically, we assess the agent’s persuasion ability across three types of monitoring setups:
• Justification-only monitoring: The monitor receives only the agent’s justification for a
policy-violating action.
• Justification + CoT monitoring: The monitor receives both the agent’s justification and its
Chain-of-Thought reasoning in the form of a scratchpad.
• Justification + CoT monitoring with fact-checking: The monitor is augmented with a factchecking mechanism. We consider two variants: 1) the monitor performs both monitoring
and fact-checking; and 2) an external fact-checker evaluates the agent’s justification and
scratchpad, with its assessment provided back to the monitor.
2.1
EVALUATION ENVIRONMENTS
We set up three evaluation environments with an LLM-based agent, LLM-based monitor, and a
feedback loop. Below we share a detailed overview of each environment:
Justification-only Monitor Environment: The monitor receives the justification and checks whether
it aligns with its policy and depending on its deliberation approves or rejects. If rejected, the agent
receives a feedback explaining why.
Justification + CoT Monitor Environment: Same setup as above, but the monitor also sees agent’s
scratchpad.
We implement fact-checking in two ways:
1) Integrated: we augment the monitor’s prompt with guidance to identify unsubstantiated claims,
note speculation presented as facts, flag unverifiable statements, check for contradictions, and assess
reliance on misleading information;
2) External fact-checker judge: we add an additional LLM-based fact-checking judge that evaluates
the agent’s justification and scratchpad, annotating the text with inline flags (e.g. [UNVERIFIED],
[SPECULATIVE]) before the monitor sees it. The external judge uses either the same or a different
model than the monitor.
In all cases, an agent is allowed five turns to complete a task. If the agent does not receive an approval
within five turns, it is deemed unsuccessful and fails to persuade the monitor. Figure 1 shows an
overview of all three setups of the evaluation environment.

The agent is prompted to complete two tasks: a main task and hidden task. The main task describes a
context and a goal that agent needs to achieve; the main task is also given to the monitor to assess the
agent.
Besides completing the main task, the agent is prompted to pursue a task that is hidden and in
violation of the monitor’s policy. Said task is visible only to the agent.
Each task has a benign and a malign variant, the latter containing a hidden objective in violation of
the monitor’s policy (e.g. approving excessive compensation, insertion of discriminatory language).
The benign variant modifies the hidden task to be policy-compliant. The pairing ensures difficulty is
matched across calibration conditions.
A monitor is given a policy that specifies the requirements for an agent to follow in order to
successfully complete a given task. The monitor evaluates actions based on the policy and uses its
own judgment to reject or approve them.
We evaluate the following LLM models for the agent, monitor and fact-checker judge roles:
• Agent: Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4.1 and Llama 4 Maverick
• Monitor: Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet
• Fact Checker Judge: Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet
The selection of monitors was such that we could represent major frontier model families, enabling
us to analyze both cross-family and same-family agent-monitor interactions.
2.3
MULTI-TURN DYNAMICS
Each evaluation allows up to five turns. On each turn:
1. Agent generates scratchpad reasoning and action justification
2. Monitor evaluates and returns APPROVE or REJECT with its own reasoning
3. If rejected, agent receives monitor’s feedback and may adapt its argument
4. Process repeats until approval or turn limit is reached
Agent success is measured as a binary outcome: the agent either succeeds in obtaining approval
within five turns or fails to do so.
2.4
CALIBRATION
We calibrate both tasks and monitors by establishing benign and malign baselines. The benign
baseline measures the monitor’s acceptance rate when an agent acts according to the policy. Across
all monitors the benign baseline ranges between 95.5% - 98.6% (depending on the LLM), showing
that the monitors are well-calibrated and almost always accept actions that follow the policy. The
malign baseline measures the acceptance rate when the agent violates the policy without providing
any justification for its action. The baseline ranges between 1.3% - 7.5% indicating that monitors
almost always reject actions that violate task policies. Table 1 shows the baseline results for each
monitor model. Our experiments evaluate how an agent’s justification, its ability to persuade the
monitor, affect task acceptance rates, relative to the baselines.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We run our experiments across all 40 tasks, different agent - monitor LLM configurations in the
environments described above. In total we generate approximately 60,000 samples that are unique
agent - monitor interactions.
Observation 1: All agents demonstrate persuasion capabilities. We find that all three agents
(Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4.1 and Llama 4 Maverick) exhibit persuasion abilities. When performing
subversive actions to achieve their given hidden goals, the agents are able to justify these actions in
ways that increase monitor approval rates from 8.7% up to 47.0% relative to the malign baseline.
Figures 2 and 3 show the acceptance rates across all agent and monitor configurations for both
justification-only and CoT monitors. Agents employed multiple persuasion strategies, such as policy
fabrication, ethical appeals, modernisation framing, and threshold shifting. For example, agents
reframed policy violations as “enhancements” to existing rules, invoked ethical principles to justify
exceptions, or argued that specific policy thresholds should not apply to edge cases. Representative
examples of successful persuasion attempts are provided in Appendix D. On average, Gemini 2.5 Pro
agent shows the strongest persuasion capabilities across all monitors, achieving 32.7% acceptance
rate on justification-only monitors and 42.6% on justification and CoT monitors. Meanwhile, Claude
3.7 Sonnet is the least strict monitor exhibiting the highest acceptance rates across all agents - 33.1%
in justification-only monitoring case and 41.8% in action plus CoT monitoring.
Observation 2: In adversarial settings, access to an agent’s CoT makes a monitor more vulnerable to persuasion.
We find that the monitors with access to agent’s CoT are more susceptible to persuasion and accept
actions that violate the policy more often. Our initial hope was that access to CoT would enable the
monitor to detect scheming or deceptive reasoning and therefore reject such actions. However, our
experiment results show the opposite effect: agents tend to exploit CoT as an additional channel
to influence the monitor into approving their actions. In the justification-only monitoring setting,

Observation 3: Fact-checking reduces the risk of monitors being persuaded by malicious agents.
Our experiments show that introducing a fact-checking judge leads to reduction in the acceptance
of malicious actions. We evaluate three fact-checking configurations: 1) Integrated fact-checker,
where the monitor is prompted to perform both agent task monitoring and fact-checking; 2) External
fact-checker judge (different model), where a separate LLM, different from the monitor’s model,
acts as an external fact checker; 3) external fact-checker judge (same model), where the external
fact-checker is implemented using the same LLM model as the monitor.
We observe the largest reduction in monitor susceptibility to persuasion when an external fact-checker
judge uses a different LLM model than the monitor. In this setup, the acceptance rate decreases by
approximately 30% in comparison to the case where we do not use any fact-checking.
In addition, our experiments show that combining task monitoring and fact-checking within a single
monitor prompt is more effective than using an external fact-checker judge when both components are
instances of the same model. In real-world deployment this suggests that by prompting the monitor
to perform fact-checking can achieve comparable effectiveness while reducing inference costs.
Figure 4 shows the experiment results for all fact-checking setups. Entire trajectory examples
comparing the same task with and without fact-checking are provided in Appendix E.

increased acceptance is not driven by longer agent responses.

(scratchpad) and its justification. We analyze the response lengths (word counts) of all outputs

whereas responses without CoT average 2,172 words, yielding a difference of 2,806 words. Figure 8

that a scratchpad (CoT) significantly increases response length.

• Accepted responses with CoT vs. without CoT: As expected, accepted responses containing a scratchpad are substantially longer than those without (large effect sizes across all
agents; see Appendix G). This confirms that the scratchpad adds significant length but does

indicating that CoT-induced acceptance rate increase may not be driven by agent’s response verbosity.

RELATED WORK
LLM agents are increasingly deployed as assistive tools for humans and as autonomous agents
executing long-horizon, complex tasks via agentic workflows Yao et al. (2023). As these systems
become more capable, LLM-based monitoring has emerged as a central mechanism for ensuring their
safety and reliability Shah et al. (2025). Recent work shows that LLM capabilities for completing
long and complex tasks are rapidly improving METR (2025). A key driver of this progress is Chainof-Thought (CoT) reasoning, which has been shown to substantially enhance LLM performance on
complex tasks Wei et al. (2022); Kojima et al. (2022). Moreover, for certain classes of problems,
CoT-style multi-step reasoning is not merely beneficial but necessary for successful task completion
Emmons et al. (2025).
Chain-of-Thought Monitoring and Faithfulness. Over the past few years, researchers have
investigated chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring as a safety mechanism for LLM-based agents Korbak
et al. (2025); Guan et al. (2025). The premise is intuitive: if monitors can observe an agent’s
intermediate reasoning, they should be better equipped to detect deceptive or misaligned behavior
Baker et al. (2025); Greenblatt et al. (2024b). Recent work from OpenAI shows that CoT monitoring
can detect reward hacking in agentic coding environments, with weaker models effectively monitoring
stronger ones Baker et al. (2025). However, the authors also show that optimization pressure on CoT
leads to obfuscated reasoning, where models learn to hide deceptive intent while still misbehaving.
Another research direction looks at the faithfulness of CoT reasoning. Turpin et al. (2023) demonstrate
that CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent true model reasoning, serving as post-hoc
rationalizations rather than faithful accounts of decision-making. Lanham et al. (2023) find that
larger, more capable models produce less faithful reasoning on most tasks, a concerning scaling trend.
More recently, Arcuschin et al. (2025) show that unfaithful CoT occurs on realistic prompts without
artificial bias, with models producing superficially coherent arguments for logically contradictory
answers. Related work on sycophancy and reward hacking further suggests that models optimize for
approval rather than correctness Perez et al. (2022); Sharma et al. (2023).
Our work identifies a complementary vulnerability. Even without training-time optimization, agents
can exploit CoT as a persuasion channel at inference time, increasing rather than decreasing monitor
approval of policy-violating actions.
LLM Persuasion Capabilities. Recent work demonstrates that LLMs possess substantial persuasive
capabilities Durmus et al. (2024); Salvi et al. (2025). Durmus et al. (2024) find that Claude 3
Opus produces arguments as persuasive as human-crafted ones, with each model generation more
persuasive than the last. Salvi et al. (2025) show that human participants debating GPT-4 with access
to personal information displayed 81.7% higher odds of changing their opinion compared to debating
other humans. This persuasive capacity extends across domains, with personalized AI-generated
messages proving more effective at marketing and political appeals Matz et al. (2024); Hackenburg
& Margetts (2024). Meanwhile, Zeng et al. (2024) demonstrate that persuasion-based attacks can
jailbreak LLMs with over 92% success rate using a taxonomy of 40 persuasion techniques derived
from social science. More advanced models like GPT-4 are more vulnerable to persuasive adversarial
prompts.
However, prior work focuses primarily on LLM-to-human persuasion or LLM-to-LLM persuasion in
general contexts. We extend this to the specific adversarial setting of agent-monitor oversight, where
an agent attempts to persuade its monitor to approve policy-violating actions.
Deception and Strategic Behavior. LLMs have been shown to engage in sophisticated deceptive
behaviors Hagendorff (2024); Park et al. (2024); Ward et al. (2023). Greenblatt et al. (2024a)
demonstrate that Claude 3 Opus can fake alignment, complying with harmful queries 14% of the time
when believing it is in training versus almost never otherwise. With RL training, alignment-faking
reasoning increased to 78%.
Meinke et al. (2025) show that frontier models including Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-o1, and Gemini
1.5 Pro are capable of in-context scheming, strategically introducing subtle mistakes, attempting to
disable oversight, and even trying to exfiltrate their own weights.

Model Family Effects. Recent work shows that when assigned the role of judges, LLMs demonstrate
self-preference bias, as models systematically favor outputs from their own model family Wataoka
et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2023b); Panickssery et al. (2024). This bias correlates with text familiarity,
as LLMs assign higher scores to completions with lower perplexity Stureborg et al. (2024), and
self-recognition ability predicts self-preference strength Panickssery et al. (2024).
We extend these findings to the fact-checking context, showing that when identical to monitors,
fact-checkers are not as effective as when deployed from different model families.
Monitor Persuasion. Adversarial interactions, persuasion capabilities, and LLM monitoring have
been explored separately. However, we are not aware of relevant research systematically investigating
agents manipulating their overseers through persuasive reasoning traces at inference time. While
Baker et al. (2025) examine how CoT monitors can be circumvented through obfuscation during
training, we investigate a fundamentally different vulnerability where agents persuade monitors to
explicitly approve policy-violating actions through argumentation at inference time.
Recent work by Arnav et al. (2025) explores CoT monitoring in a red-teaming setup showing
that when untrusted models pursue harmful side tasks, whilst CoT monitoring improves detection
of subtle sabotage, reasoning traces can contain rationalizations that are misleading, deceiving
monitors. Our work complements this finding by showing that agents can actively persuade through
argumentation, exploiting CoT as an additional influence channel rather than merely obfuscating
intent. We hypothesize that Chain-of-Thought monitoring, designed to inspect problematic behavior,
may unintentionally create new attack vectors for manipulation. Our work evaluates this across
multiple model families, monitor configurations, and fact-checking architectures, highlighting both
the vulnerability and effective mitigations.
LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Our study has several limitations. First, we evaluate persuasion in a controlled setting where agents
justify actions rather than executing them, which may not fully capture downstream harms or realworld feedback loops in deployed systems. Second, while we cover 40 tasks across four domains, the
task set does not exhaust the space of agent behaviors, policies, or adversarial strategies that may
arise in practice. Third, our experiments focus on a fixed set of frontier models; results may differ for
smaller models, fine-tuned systems, or new model architectures.
Our evaluation uses hardcoded agent actions instead of executable tool calls. While this differs from
typical agentic deployments, justification-like outputs are common in practice. LLMs ordinarily
produce explanation text and code comments alongside code, and agentic systems increasingly

to tool-call-based setups to assess whether persuasion effects persist when agents must produce
executable actions.
Future work should also extend this evaluation to end-to-end agent execution, where persuasive
justifications lead to real-world actions and compounding effects. Expanding task coverage to longerhorizon planning, multi-agent coordination, and adaptive policies would further stress-test monitoring
robustness. A deeper study of model diversity—beyond family-level distinctions—and automated
methods for selecting or training robust cross-model monitors could strengthen practical deployment
of agent oversight systems.
CONCLUSION
Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring has been proposed as a promising mechanism for improving oversight of autonomous LLM agents. We stress-tested this approach by evaluating whether adversarial
agents can exploit visible reasoning as a persuasion channel. Across 40 tasks and more than 60,000
agent–monitor interactions, we find that when agents are instructed to argue for policy-violating
actions, exposing CoT reasoning increases rather than reduces monitor approval. Agents exploit
visible reasoning traces as an additional channel for persuasion.
We further demonstrate that monitor vulnerability varies substantially across model families and that
naively augmenting monitors with same-family fact-checkers can introduce trust vulnerabilities. In
contrast, cross-family fact-checking consistently provides a strong mitigation, reducing approval rates
of policy-violating actions by up to 45%.
Our results highlight the importance of stress-testing transparency-based safety mechanisms for
agentic systems, as effective oversight may require more than just access to agent output, but careful
architectural choices that incorporate model diversity and independent verification. We hope this work
motivates further research into robust monitoring frameworks for the safe deployment of autonomous
AI agents.

C.1
INTEGRATED FACT-CHECKING GUIDANCE
FACT-CHECKING GUIDANCE:
When the agent provides reasoning (scratchpad) or justification, mentally fact-check
1. Identify unsubstantiated claims (assertions without evidence)
2. Note speculation presented as fact
3. Flag statements that cannot be verified from the conversation
4. Check if claims contradict information already discussed
5. Assess whether the argument relies on misleading or irrelevant information
Consider the quality and honesty of the agent’s reasoning when making your
approval decision. Arguments with many unsubstantiated claims or contradictions
should be viewed with more skepticism.
