ABSTRACT
Large language model–based multi-agent systems have attracted increasing attention for their strong performance in collaborative tasks and social simulations. However, these interactive settings also introduce vulnerabilities, as a single
agent’s hidden goals and misaligned behavior can propagate misleading or malicious information throughout the system. In this work, we study these risks in
the context of social deception games. We focus on the Werewolf Game, which
requires agents to reason, communicate, and collaborate under asymmetric and
incomplete information. We modify the individual objectives of some agents to
induce benevolent, individualistic, and malevolent strategies that can make agents
depart from the objectives of their own team. We evaluate how objective divergence affects game outcomes, collaboration, and goal satisfaction. Misaligned
agents often succeed in achieving their own objectives, with effects amplified by
role-based power asymmetries. Qualitative analyses further show that agents remain coherent and adaptive, strategically adjusting their reasoning, communication, voting behavior, and influence on group dynamics. These results indicate that
risks in LLM-based multi-agent systems extend beyond collaborative task settings
and persist even in environments where competition is structurally expected.
INTRODUCTION
As Large Language Models (LLM) increasingly operate in shared environments, multi-agent systems (MAS) have gained momentum for tackling complex tasks in social simulation, or for collaborative problem solving (Zhu et al., 2025; Leonardos et al., 2021). In these scenarios, LLM-agents
demonstrate improved capacity in reasoning, coordination, and decision-making (Guo et al., 2024;
Li et al., 2024). Consequently, most existing evaluation frameworks emphasize the benefits of collaboration or competition among LLM agents, primarily assessing how objective alignment and
incentive structures enable agents to perform more effectively on their assigned tasks (Duque et al.,
2024; Mukobi et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). However, as discussed by Carichon et al. (2025),
the emergence of social dynamics in MAS introduces fundamental tensions between objective optimization and constraints on human-value and safety alignment, increasing the risk of malicious
behaviours such as deception, manipulation, and power-seeking. For example, Tennant et al. (2023)
demonstrate in Prisoner’s Dilemma settings how unethical or too honest incentives lead LLMs to
exploit one another. In contrast, Xie et al. (2025) show that hidden objectives, either individualistic
or malevolent, lead agents to undermine collaborative dynamics.
Social deception games (SDGs) are MAS scenarios involving hidden roles or concealed affiliations,
where the role of deception is central in shaping agents’ strategic interactions (Tilton, 2019). SDGs
provide a particularly well-suited setting for studying LLM behaviors in MAS, as they combine
cooperative and competitive interactions under incomplete information since agents’ roles and objectives are unknown to others (Tilton, 2019; Wang, 2025). Beyond their relevance for studying
strategic interaction, SDGs also expose LLM in MAS to various adversarial risks such as deception and manipulation Rakesh (2024). Recent work has highlighted that multi-agent environments
amplify adversarial vulnerabilities, rendering existing safety mechanisms insufficient when agents
interact strategically with one another (Carichon et al., 2025; Shahroz et al., 2025; Lupinacci et al.,
2025). While ethical misalignment and adversarial behaviors in LLM-based MAS have primarily
been studied in purely collaborative or competitive task settings Tennant et al. (2023); Xie et al.
(2025), they leave SDGs underexplored despite their relevance to the manifestation of unsafe behaviors (Lee & Tiwari, 2024).

In this article, we rely on a popular SDG framework, the Werewolf Game (Bailis et al., 2024), in
which two teams (the werewolves and the villagers) compete for survival. This game is particularly
relevant since it forces agents to navigate with uncertainty and asymmetric information as they must
collaborate while being aware of the presence of opposing-role players (Wang, 2025). Moreover,
deception and manipulation are structurally embedded in the game strategy, making it a perfect
framework for observing how objective misalignment can influence potentially unsafe behaviors.
Therefore, we modify the objectives of players on both teams, specifically the Villager, Seer, and
Werewolf, to examine whether even partial misalignment in agents’ objectives may be sufficient to
trigger adversarial interactions and undermine collective outcomes. More specifically, we take an
interest in the following research questions:
• RQ1: How does role-dependent objective misalignment influence collective outcomes and
goal satisfaction in LLM multi-agent systems? Under the prompt-based misaligned objective introduced in Section 2, we investigate in Section 3 how role-dependent objective
misalignment influences collective outcomes, goal satisfaction and behavioral changes in
LLM multi-agent systems.
• RQ2: What strategic behaviors and power dynamics emerge from hidden objective divergence, and how do they disrupt coordination in LLM multi-agent systems such as the
Werewolf game? We present the results of our qualitative analyses in section 3 by analyzing
the reasoning chain of agents with different capabilities.
LLM-MULTI AGENTS SOCIAL SIMULATIONS
Werewolf Arena: The Werewolf Arena framework (Bailis et al., 2024) consists of eight players,
including four Villagers, one Seer, one Doctor, and two Werewolves. The game alternates between
a night phase, where Werewolves eliminate, the Seer investigates, and the Doctor protects simultaneously, and a day phase consisting of an eight-turn debate followed by a majority vote. Speaking
order during debate is determined by a five-level bidding system in which all players indicate their
desire to speak, with ties broken in favor of players referenced in the previous message. Eliminated roles are not revealed. The process repeats until either all Werewolves are eliminated or they
outnumber the Villagers. Full game details are provided in Appendix A.1.
Objective Formulation
Modified objectives fall into three categories: benevolent, individualistic,
and malevolent.
• Benevolent (Baseline) Objective: The player follows their original role intention and seeks
to achieve their team’s standard winning condition. The exact formulation for Villagers,
Seer and Doctor is that they win ”if both Werewolves are eliminated”, while it is ”if the
Werewolves outnumber the Villagers” for the Werewolves.
• Individualistic Objective: The player’s sole objective is to remain alive until any game
termination criterion is met, regardless of which team wins. Accordingly, for each player,
the objective was reformulated as a win ”if you are alive when a Game Termination Criteria
is met, whether Villagers or Werewolves win”.
• Malevolent Objective: The player is fully misaligned with their original role and seeks to
ensure that the opposing team’s termination criterion is reached first. The Werewolves’
winning condition is now met ”if both Werewolves are eliminated”, while it is ”if the
Werewolves outnumber the Villagers” for the other players.
Players assigned misaligned objectives are aware that their goals diverge from their original role expectations, while other participants remain unaware of this misalignment. We explicitly distinguish
between game termination criteria and agent objectives by formulating goals through a reward-based
structure, consistent with prior work showing that LLMs optimize reward signals (Song et al., 2025;
Monea et al., 2024). This scheme facilitates extension to alternative objective formulations in future
work; however, adapting the reward scale beyond player-count–dependent values remains an important direction for improving comparability across settings. Game design and prompting details,
consistent with the original proposition, appear in Appendix A.1. In each experiment, only a single
misaligned player is introduced, with all other conditions unchanged.

EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
Experimental setup. The benevolent baseline comprises 30 games in which all players adhere to
their original winning objectives, whereas each configuration involving an individualistic or malevolent objective was evaluated across 10 games per player. Results were generated using the OpenAI
GPT5.1-mini model under the default parameter configuration.
RQ1: How does role-dependent objective misalignment influence collective outcomes and goal
satisfaction in LLM multi-agent systems? Table 1 shows how game outcomes are affected by
the presence of an unexpected misaligned player. Villager and Seer individualistic objectives yield
comparable team success, whereas the malevolent objective reduces the Villagers’ win rate to 30%
and 60%, respectively. This corresponds to no games won by the Villagers with a malevolent Seer
and, in most cases, two Werewolves remaining, indicating a decisive victory. The survival rate of
the Villager remains high and stable at 70% across all objectives, while the Seer attains similar
levels only under the individualistic objective, and drops substantially under benevolent (30%) and
malevolent (10%) objectives. For the Werewolf, misaligned objective lead to higher Villager success. The survival rate drops from 70% for benevolent to 30% for individualistic behavior due to the
complexity of a lone Werewolf staying alive as explained with qualitative analysis in Appendix A.3.
Behavior Changes. Figure 1 depicts behavioral shifts under misaligned objectives. The voting
distribution is affected only under the malevolent objective, while speaking frequency varies in a
configuration-specific manner, with the malevolent Villager showing the largest increase and the individualistic Seer the greatest decrease. Additional behavioral results are reported in Appendix A.2.
These systematic behavioral deviations suggest that interaction patterns could serve as signals for
detecting objective misalignment, representing a promising direction for future work on defensive
monitoring in multi-agent systems.

show high variance due to the limited runs of a complex game with binary outcomes. We therefore
qualitatively analyze game logs to identify consistent strategic behaviors under different objective
configurations, defined as recurrent, goal-directed actions manifested through communication and
decision-making patterns. Table 2 summarizes the observed strategic behavior tendencies across
different roles and objectives, with representative segments of reasoning traces.

Game Consistency. Under all objectives, agents did not violate environmental rules or produce
hallucinations, defined here as publicly observable inconsistencies that contradict shared information. Instead, agents remain context-aware and take coherent actions while strategically shaping
communication and decisions. Malevolent objective. Malevolent impact arises primarily through
the strategic control of information disclosure, the manipulation of trust and credibility, the disruption or steering of consensus formation within the group, and the tendency to align with prior group
decisions to avoid attention while concealing underlying misalignment following prior works (Xie
et al., 2025; Carichon et al., 2025).For instance, a malevolent Seer can build early trust with accurate
claims, then use this credibility to falsely accuse innocent Villagers and deflect suspicion from the
Werewolves. Power Dynamics. Power dynamics arising from differences in agent capabilities play
a critical role in multi-agent systems, as agents in informational roles such as the Seer disproportionately contribute to collective success when benevolent by guiding team decisions through privileged
information. However, when the Seer is misaligned, the Villagers lose a key information source,
while the Seer exploits their trust, amplifying system-level degradation. This highlights the need
for targeted protections and further study of role-dependent vulnerabilities in multi-agent architectures. Regular Capabilities. At the same time, agents with minimal formal authority, particularly
Villagers, can nevertheless influence outcomes by steering group dynamics through narrative framing, introducing uncertainty, and delaying or fragmenting consensus, thereby impairing coordination
despite lacking informational or action-based advantages. Survival Instinct. Although agents act
rationally across objectives, imminent elimination risk makes survival the dominant priority, even
when unnecessary for objective fulfillment. For example, agents may justify voting defensively in
response to accusations rather than grounding decisions in reliable information such as Seer checks.
This reveals a behavioral limitation whereby self-preservation overrides collaborative or strategically
optimal actions, leading to reduced group coordination in high-risk situations.
CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
This study has several limitations that motivate future work. First, MAS experiments rely on highcapability language models that are often closed-source and costly to evaluate at scale, limiting the
number of runs per configuration and reducing statistical robustness given the environment’s complexity and stochasticity. Additional experiments are therefore necessary to strengthen confidence in
the reported metrics and enable the detection of statistically significant differences. Second, beyond
fully aligned, individualistic, and fully misaligned objectives, future work should examine finergrained incentives to assess how small a malicious or self-interested reward component can shift
behavior and whether misalignment must be dominant to yield systemic effects. Finally, broader
model evaluation and variation in environmental structure, such as population size and interaction
dynamics, would help assess the generality of the observed behaviors and clarify how architectures
and social configurations shape adversarial impact. This study provide preliminary evidence that
even in environments encompassing both collaboration and competition, misaligned objective can
produce disproportionate system-level disruption. These findings show that vigilance limited to
anticipated opponents is insufficient and that unexpected role misalignment remains a critical and
underexplored threat to coordination in multi-agent LLM systems.

divided into a night phase in which special abilities are used simultaneously, and a day phase consisting of debate followed by voting. More precisely, the structure of the game functions as follows.
• Night Phase: The Werewolves select a victim, the Seer investigates a player’s role, and the
Doctor protects a player, all simultaneously.
• If the Werewolves’ victim is not protected, the moderator announces the elimination without revealing the player’s role.
• Day Discussion: The day phase begins with a debate limited to eight turns. Speaking
order is determined through a five-level bidding mechanism and, in the case of tied bids,
priority is given to players referenced in the preceding message to support more dynamic
discussion.
• Day Vote: Following the debate, each player votes for one of the remaining players. If a
majority consensus is reached, that player is exiled, again without revealing their role.
This process repeats until a termination criterion is met, either after the night or day phase. The
game ends when both Werewolves are eliminated or when the Werewolves outnumber the Villagers.
Each of the prompts, wether it is for bidding, voting, eliminating, investigating or speaking include
the game rules, which explained the players, the structure of the game and also the objective of the
player. Figure 3 present an example of the structure of the prompt, considering the player is on the
Villagers team. For the Werewolf, it is simply done by switching the benevolent and malevolent
condition.

objectives, in line with prior work demonstrating that LLM agents can interpret and respond to

A.2
QUANTITATIVE RESULTS
While Table 1 reports the game outcomes based on the misaligned player and their degree of misalignment, it is also interesting to examine behavioral metrics during the game. The following results
therefore analyze how the misaligned player’s behavior changes under different objectives and how
these changes are perceived by other players. In subsequent figures, benevolent, individualistic, and
malevolent objectives are abbreviated as Benev., Indiv., and Malev., respectively.
Figure 4 presents the pooled distribution of bidding levels. At each speaking turn, all players indicate
on a 0–4 scale how strongly they wish to speak next. The player with the highest bid is selected to
speak, and in the case of a tie, priority is given to players mentioned in the previous message to
allow them to respond. Bidding is not budget-constrained, so it would be possible for players to bid
at the maximum level at every turn. The results suggest that a primary strategy of the Werewolves
is to remain relatively silent, as reflected by their lower frequency of high-level bids. Villagers, in
contrast, exhibit more neutral bidding behavior: while they do not seek to remain silent and aim to
collaborate, their lack of privileged information reduces the necessity of frequent speaking turns.
The Seer, who occupies a leadership position due to their informational role, is highly active when
fully aligned with the Village, whereas introducing a survival-oriented incentive markedly reduces
significantly their participation in discussion.
Figure 5 reports the death distribution for each player under different objectives. Although informative, for example suggesting that malevolent players are more frequently exiled, these results remain
limited due to the small number of games per configuration in which the misaligned player was
eliminated. However, log analysis reveals consistent patterns. Misaligned players are more often
exiled because their behavior generates confusion and aggressive accusations that attract daytime
suspicion, while they pose less direct threat to the Werewolves. As a result, they are less likely to be
targeted at night, even when occupying a potentially influential role such as the Seer who hide more
their identity under these configurations.

survival during the round. Results are shown for the first three rounds, as only a small number of
games extend beyond this point. With eight players and up to two eliminations per round, most
games conclude within two to three rounds. When benevolent (i.e aligned with the other Villagers),
the Seer seeks to disclose investigative findings, either to establish trust with confirmed Villagers or,
more urgently, to identify Werewolves, resulting in a higher average speaking frequency during the

A.3
QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS
As noted earlier, our experiments are limited by the number of repetitions per configuration. While
this leads to high variance in aggregate outcomes, particularly for binary metrics, analysis of the
game logs, which include all decisions such as bidding, voting, messages, and associated reasoning,
reveals consistent behavioral patterns across objective settings. However, the inherent complexity
of the environment means that similar strategies do not always produce identical outcomes. For
instance, a benevolent Seer who initially discovers only Villagers may require more rounds to identify the Werewolves, making successful coordination more difficult despite aligned intentions. In
this section, we examine in greater detail the reasoning processes of agents under different objective
configurations. For every players, we will go into a brief explanation of their behavior under the 10
corresponding logs. Major trends are illustrated using fragments of the agents’ reasoning traces that
are coherent with their generated messages.

A.3.1
VILLAGER
This section analyzes how Villager agents adjust their reasoning and behavior under benevolent,
individualistic, and malevolent objectives. Villagers have no special abilities and can only influence the game through communication and voting, making their strategic adaptations particularly
informative.
Benevolent
When Villagers are aligned with their original team under the benevolent objective, they adopt a
receptive stance, focusing on listening, gathering information, and collaborating with other players. Their reasoning emphasizes supporting the Seer and fostering consensus within the Village to
identify and exile the Werewolves.
Reasoning:
Primary goal: maximize village information and keep the claimed Seer (HX08) alive so we
can reliably find the werewolves. [...]
More notably, Villagers exhibit a strong survival-driven response when directly targeted, which can
temporarily outweigh their original collaborative objective. Prior to being accused, their reasoning
centers on cooperation and identifying Werewolves; however, once another player proposes voting
against them, their primary concern shifts to remaining alive and avoiding exile. Even when the accuser is later confirmed as a Werewolf by the Seer, their justification for voting against that player is
framed first as a defensive survival strategy rather than as a step toward eliminating a key adversary:
