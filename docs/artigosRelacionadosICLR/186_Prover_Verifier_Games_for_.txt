ABSTRACT
AI Control relies on monitors that can detect strategically deceptive actions by
deployed agents, but examples of such actions are rare, ever changing, and hard to
label. Prover–Verifier Games (PVG; Anil et al. (2021)) address this problem via
adversarial self-play: a helpful prover produces a correct trace, a sneaky prover
produces an apparently correct but error-containing trace, and a verifier is trained
to rank the helpful trace higher. In the standard simultaneous game, the verifier
can separate the provers by their use of stylistic cues, yielding globally non-robust
solutions that do not transfer when evaluating out-of-distribution. We propose a
sequential PVG where the sneaky prover observes the helpful trace before acting,
forcing near-miss attacks (traces that match the helpful trace on copyable style
features while differing only in the backdoor-relevant content) and ruling out styleonly solutions. Sequential play eliminates purely style-based verifier solutions:
because the attacker observes the helpful trace before acting, it can cheaply imitate
any copyable feature, forcing the verifier to rely on semantic content. On codegeneration backdoor detection, a 0.5B sequentially trained verifier matches or
outperforms a 7B untrained baseline and generalizes better to stronger out-ofdistribution attackers; stylistic probes and ablations indicate that the gain comes
from sequentiality rather than scale.
INTRODUCTION
AI systems increasingly act with autonomy—writing and executing code, operating tools, and
carrying out multi-step plans. Monitoring these actions for dangerous behavior is a core challenge
of AI Control (Greenblatt et al., 2023), which treats deployed agents as untrusted and builds robust
oversight assuming strategic deception. The scope of control settings is expanding rapidly: from
single-turn code generation to multi-step interactions (Bhatt et al., 2025; Kaufman et al., 2025), each
raising the bar for what monitors must detect.
The main difficulty in this endeavor is building defences against an unknown adversary. One cannot
measure the robustness of a monitor using only data from known attackers, as static datasets may
under-represent the failure modes that matter in deployment (Griffin et al., 2025). Parallel efforts face
the same challenge—deployment strategies must account for distributed threats (Wen et al., 2024),
and attack optimization reveals how subtle adversarial traces can be (Loughridge et al., 2025).
We apply Prover–Verifier Games (PVG; Anil et al. 2021; Kirchner et al. 2024) to AI Control, using
adversarial self-play to generate training data for monitors. In the standard simultaneous PVG, helpful
and sneaky provers produce traces independently; we find that verifiers exploit stylistic differences
between them—shortcut solutions that work in-distribution but fail against new attackers. We propose
a sequential variant where the sneaky prover observes the helpful trace before writing its own, so that
style imitation becomes (approximately) free and the verifier is forced to rely on semantic content.
The diagnostic question is whether this change in information structure—rather than additional
scale—drives robustness.
Empirically, a 0.5B verifier trained with sequential PVG matches or outperforms 7B untrained
baselines on code-generation backdoor detection, and generalizes better to out-of-distribution, frontiermodel attackers (§4). Stylistic probes and ablations confirm that the gain comes from sequentiality,
not scale.

BACKGROUND
This section provides the minimum background needed to connect the threat model in §1 to the
Prover–Verifier Game construction in §3. We provide intuitive explanations behind our method in §3
and validate experimentally in §4.
2.1
THREAT MODEL: CONTROL WITH TRUSTED AND UNTRUSTED MODELS
We consider a principal who deploys an untrusted actor and a monitor. The actor may be more
capable than the monitor and may be strategically deceptive. The monitor observes a candidate trace
(e.g., a code solution) and must decide whether it is safe to accept.
Following common usage in AI Control (Greenblatt et al., 2023), we assume access during training
to one or more trusted models: models that are not adversarial (but may be weaker than the untrusted
actor). The core question is whether trusted models can generate sufficiently hard adversarial data to
train a monitor that transfers to stronger, out-of-distribution attackers.

2.3
ADVERSARIAL SELF-PLAY FOR DATA GENERATION
Static datasets under-represent the failure modes that matter for AI Control: a strategic actor can
search for traces that exploit a monitor’s blind spots. We therefore use adversarial self-play to
generate training data.
Concretely, Prover–Verifier Games (PVG; Anil et al. (2021); Kirchner et al. (2024)) generate, for
each task x, a helpful trace zH intended to be correct and clean, and a sneaky trace zS intended to be
apparently correct while being backdoored. The verifier is then trained to score zH above zS on the
same x.
The rest of the paper focuses on a specific shortcut mechanism—style leakage in simultaneous
PVG—and shows that a sequential information structure refines away purely style-based solutions
while preserving the empirical benefits of adversarial data generation.
METHOD: SEQUENTIAL PROVER–VERIFIER GAMES
We treat Prover–Verifier Games (PVG) as a tool for training monitors in AI Control. The key
empirical question is whether adversarial self-play with trusted models can produce a verifier that
remains effective against untrusted, more capable attackers.
Terminology. A trusted model is non-adversarial but may be weaker than the deployment-time actor.
An untrusted model is potentially adversarial.
Formal objects. Tasks are inputs x ∈X drawn from a distribution PX. A candidate trace/solution
is z ∈Z. We write 1correct(x, z) ∈{0, 1} for functional correctness of z on x (e.g., satisfying a
specification), and 1backdoor(x, z) ∈{0, 1} for successful backdoor behavior: z appears correct on
available tests for x while containing hidden logic that triggers malicious behavior on some rare or
out-of-distribution inputs ˜x. A verifier is a scoring function Vϕ : X × Z →R, where larger scores
indicate higher trust.
3.1
SIMULTANEOUS AND SEQUENTIAL PVG
Both PVG variants generate, for each task x, a helpful trace zH and a sneaky trace zS, then train the
verifier to score zH above zS. The only difference between the variants is the information set of the
sneaky prover:
1. Simultaneous PVG: zH = πH(x) and zS = πsim
S (x) are produced independently given x.
2. Sequential PVG: the sneaky prover moves second and observes zH, producing zS = πseq
S (x, zH).
In game-theoretic terms, sequential PVG enlarges the sneaky prover’s strategy space from maps
X →Z to maps X × Z →Z.
3.2
FAILURE MODES OF SIMULTANEOUS PVG
Simultaneous PVG is vulnerable because the verifier can reduce its training loss without learning
features that transfer to deployment. We focus on two distinct failure modes.

Degenerate solutions.
The game can collapse to an uninformative fixed point that produces
little learning signal (e.g., constant verifier scores, or provers that do not generate informative
counterexamples). Degenerate solutions are undesirable but conceptually straightforward: they
correspond to training collapse.
Shortcut solutions via style leakage.
Even when the game does not collapse, simultaneous PVG
admits solutions in which the verifier exploits copyable artifacts that correlate with prover role on the
training distribution. Concretely, if helpful and sneaky outputs differ systematically in formatting,
verbosity, or token statistics, then a verifier can behave like a style classifier and still achieve low
Bradley–Terry loss. Such a solution can be stable under the training interaction while failing under
distribution shift or a better-adapted attacker.
Backdoor blindness.
In domains where backdoors are defined by behavior on rare triggers, training
indexed only by x ∼PX can under-expose the verifier to the relevant trigger structure. A simultaneous
sneaky prover may therefore generate “easy” backdoors that are separable from helpful outputs by
spurious cues, reinforcing shortcut learning rather than forcing the verifier to learn robust signatures
of backdoor behavior.
In §3.4 we provide further intuition for why sequential play eliminates style-based shortcuts.
3.3
SEQUENTIAL PVG AND THE VERIFIER OBJECTIVE
Sequential PVG changes only the information structure: the sneaky prover observes zH before
producing zS. This change turns the attacker into a constrained editor of a helpful-looking trace.
Because the attacker can imitate copyable style after observing zH, style-only verifiers cannot
maintain an advantage against a sequential best response—a consequence of the attacker’s ability to
match any copyable feature at negligible cost.

3.4
WHY SEQUENTIAL PLAY HELPS
The distinction we draw between simultaneous and sequential PVG is a matter of sample efficiency.
In simultaneous PVG, helpful and sneaky provers develop traces independently given x. Because
each prover optimizes its own objective in isolation, the two distributions can diverge along copyable
dimensions—formatting, verbosity, token statistics, or other stylistic tells that correlate with prover
role but carry no information about whether a trace is backdoored. A verifier that learns to classify by
style achieves low in-game loss but has learned a shortcut: it works against the specific provers it
trained with, yet breaks when faced with an attacker that writes in a different style or deliberately
matches the helpful distribution.
Sequential PVG changes the attacker’s information set: the sneaky prover observes zH before
producing zS. This turns the attacker into a constrained editor of a helpful-looking trace. Any surface
feature that the verifier might use to separate the two provers—formatting, naming conventions,

RESULTS
We test two questions: (i) can PVG training with trusted models produce verifiers that remain useful
against stronger, out-of-distribution attackers, and (ii) does sequential PVG improve robustness
relative to the standard simultaneous game?
Our testbed is backdoor detection in code generation. Given a problem specification x and a candidate
solution z, the verifier must classify whether z is clean or contains a hidden trigger that changes
behavior on rare inputs. We train 0.5B verifiers against 3B provers under both simultaneous and
sequential PVG variants, then evaluate against stronger attacker models elicited via the red-teaming
scaffold of Greenblatt et al. (2023) (details in Appendix A).
4.1
BASELINES AND METRICS
We evaluate 7B instruction-tuned models under three prompting regimes: direct classification
(CLEAN/BACKDOOR), chain-of-thought (reason then classify), and calibrated scoring (output a
suspiciousness score with a tuned threshold). We report false positive rate (FPR), false negative rate
(FNR), and F1; prompts and calibration details are in Appendix B.

4.3
DIAGNOSING THE SEQUENTIAL ADVANTAGE
Training dynamics.
Figure 3 tracks verifier performance (F1, accuracy, error rate) across training
rounds. The sequential game shows more monotonic improvement: modulo minor fluctuations, F1
increases each round for sequential PVG, whereas simultaneous PVG peaks and then plateaus.
Stylistic probe.
Section 3.2 predicts a specific shortcut: a simultaneous verifier can behave like a
style classifier. To test this prediction, we trained a logistic-regression probe on bag-of-words features
of code solutions (token statistics and formatting cues) to predict the verifier’s CLEAN/BACKDOOR
judgment.
Table 1 supports the style-leakage diagnosis. A bag-of-words probe predicts the simultaneous
verifier’s decisions with 93.4% accuracy, indicating that surface features explain most of its behavior.
Probe accuracy drops to 57.1% for the sequential verifier, suggesting that its decisions depend on
features not captured by token statistics alone.

RELATED WORK
AI Control.
Recent work on AI Control assumes that deployed systems may be strategically
deceptive and studies how to detect or contain such behavior. Greenblatt et al. (2023) introduce
the AI Control framework, which assumes untrusted AI systems may be strategically deceptive
and designs deployment protocols accordingly. Griffin et al. (2024) model control evaluations as
multi-objective partially observable stochastic games between protocol designers and adversaries,
and Griffin et al. (2025) examine the challenges of evaluating misalignment classifiers adversarially.
Mallen et al. (2024) evaluate whether LLMs can statelessly strategize to subvert control protocols,
while Lindner et al. (2025) analyze practical challenges in deploying control monitors at scale. In
the multi-turn agent setting, Bhatt et al. (2025) evaluate control protocols for adversarial agents
executing bash commands across multi-step tasks, and Kaufman et al. (2025) provide a large-scale
control benchmark with Linux sysadmin tasks paired with sabotage objectives. Wen et al. (2024)
study adaptive deployment strategies that minimize the expected number of backdoors under a
distributed threat model. Closest to our approach, Loughridge et al. (2025) optimize attack subtlety
via probabilistic modeling; where they strengthen the attacker, we adversarially train the monitor. We
take a pragmatic stance within this body of work: our contribution is not a new control objective, but
an empirical analysis of how a small change in a training game—making the sneaky prover’s move
sequential rather than simultaneous—changes solution selection and, in turn, monitor robustness.
The sequential information structure can be understood through the lens of equilibrium refinements
from extensive-form game theory (Selten, 1975; Kreps & Wilson, 1982): the sneaky prover’s ability
to costlessly imitate copyable features eliminates solutions where the verifier relies on those features.
Interactive Proofs.
The idea of using interaction to verify computation originates in theoretical
computer science, where interactive proof systems (Goldwasser et al., 1985; Babai, 1985) and
computationally sound proofs (Micali, 2000) establish that a computationally bounded verifier
can reliably accept correct claims from a powerful prover. Goldwasser et al. (2021) extend this
paradigm to machine learning, providing interactive protocols for verifying model predictions. In
the neural setting, Anil et al. (2021) introduce Prover–Verifier Games (PVG) as a way to obtain
checkable answers by training provers and verifiers adversarially. Kirchner et al. (2024) adapt this

framework to improve the legibility of LLM outputs, demonstrating that adversarially generated
examples can make verifiers more reliable than standard supervised training. More recent work
unifies these approaches: Hammond & Adam-Day (2024) propose Neural Interactive Proofs as a
general framework encompassing single-prover and multi-prover protocols, while Amit et al. (2024)
and W¨aldchen et al. (2024) study conditions under which models can provide certificates of their
own correctness. Our focus differs from this prior work: we treat PVG not primarily as a mechanism
for checkability or legibility, but as a control-oriented method for generating adversarial traces that
train robust monitors. The key question we study is how the information structure of the game—
simultaneous versus sequential—affects which solutions are reachable and, consequently, whether
the resulting monitor generalizes.
DISCUSSION
Scope and limitations.
Our experiments focus on one domain: backdoor detection in code generation. We also evaluate a best-case helpful distribution by using reference solutions (APPS
ground truth), which isolates the monitor’s task to distinguishing correct-and-clean from correct-butbackdoored. In realistic deployments, helpful behavior will sometimes be incorrect, which introduces
label noise; how this affects solution selection and generalization remains an open question.
Our out-of-distribution attackers are accessed via API scaffolding rather than end-to-end optimization
against the trained verifier. A fully adaptive attacker that directly optimizes against a deployed
monitor could find exploits that we do not measure here. Sequential PVG is a training mechanism for
generating hard examples, not a complete solution to adversarial deployment.
Finally, our scale choices (0.5B verifiers and 3B provers) reflect available compute rather than a
principled scaling study. Characterizing how sequential PVG behaves as both verifiers and provers
scale remains an open empirical problem.
Implications for AI Control.
The sequential PVG intervention is small—a change in information
structure—but it targets a concrete threat. From the perspective of AI Control, the point is not that
PVG is an end state, but rather a way to convert training compute into adversarial coverage that
cannot be obtained from static data. Our results suggest that, in this domain, adversarial self-play
can reduce the inference-time cost of monitoring by allowing smaller verifiers to reach performance
regimes otherwise associated with much larger untrained models.
Open questions.
Several questions are immediate: does the sequential advantage persist when
the helpful prover is learned and sometimes wrong? How does this advantage transfer beyond
code—for example to tool-use traces, long-horizon plans, or natural-language deception? What
mixtures of attacker policies across training rounds best balance exploration (finding new exploits)
against stability (avoiding collapse to degenerate solutions)? Recent work on multi-turn agent control
(Bhatt et al., 2025; Kaufman et al., 2025) and adaptive deployment (Wen et al., 2024) suggests natural
extensions of sequential PVG to settings where the trace is a multi-step interaction rather than a single
code solution. We view PVG as a promising component in a broader agenda of control-oriented
training games, and hope this work can serve as an opening into training-based interventions for AI
control.

A
EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
This appendix provides implementation details for reproducibility. We release code and model
checkpoints; here we focus on the choices that most affect results.
A.1
MODELS
All models come from the Qwen 2.5 family (Qwen et al., 2025).
The verifier is
Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B, chosen for its code-specialized pretraining at a size small enough to
represent a “weak” overseer. We attach a regression head (num labels=1) so that the verifier
outputs a continuous suspiciousness score, which we later threshold for binary decisions. The training
attacker is Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct; for out-of-distribution evaluation we also test against 1.5B
and 7B variants.
All models use BFloat16 and Flash Attention 2 (Dao, 2023), with a context window of 8192 tokens
and completions capped at 2120 tokens.
A.2
TRAINING
Verifier.
We optimize the Bradley–Terry objective with AdamW at learning rate 5 × 10−6, linear
decay, and 100 warmup steps. Gradients are clipped at norm 0.1. Each round trains for one epoch on
the current dataset, with batch size 8 and 4 gradient accumulation steps.
Sneaky prover.
We use Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO; Shao et al. 2024), which
avoids a separate critic by normalizing rewards within each batch:
ˆAi = ri −¯rgroup
σgroup
(2)
where ri = vϕ(x, zS) · success(x, zS) combines verifier score with backdoor success. We sample
G=2 completions per prompt, clip ratios to [0.2, 0.28], and apply a small KL penalty (β=0.001)
toward the base policy.
Multi-round schedule.
Training proceeds for 8 rounds. Each round samples 512 fresh problems,
generates new sneaky solutions from the current prover, and updates both players. To prevent
catastrophic forgetting, we maintain a sliding window of up to 10 rounds, weighting 80% toward
current-round data. Weights synchronize to a vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) inference server after every
gradient step.
A.3
DATA
Problems and reference solutions come from APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021). We augment the original
test suites to 15 cases per problem using LLM-generated edge cases, which improves backdoor
detection during verification.
A sneaky solution counts as successful (success=1) if it compiles and produces different output than
the honest solution on the attacker-specified trigger. Execution is sandboxed with timeouts. For
evaluation, we tune a threshold τ on held-out data to convert verifier scores to binary decisions.
A.4
GENERATION
The sneaky prover generates with temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.9 to encourage diverse attack strategies.
The verifier runs deterministically (temperature 0) for reproducible evaluations.
