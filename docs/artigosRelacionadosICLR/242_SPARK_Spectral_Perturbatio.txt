ABSTRACT
Knowledge Graph based Retrieval Augmented Generation (KG-RAG) agents enhance language models by leveraging structured knowledge for improved reasoning and factual accuracy. However, their security remains largely unexplored.
In this work, we present a systematic vulnerability analysis via Graph Signatures constructed using the Haar Laplacian of the subgraph post retrieval, which
exploit spectral perturbations with minimal budget to degrade the performance.
Our framework assumes no access to the entire graph and simply functions with
read-only access to the query specific subgraph, highlighting its practicality in
all real-world applications. With extensive empirical evaluation across six KGRAG based QA datasets, we show that our attack framework induces significant
degradation in the performance, with upto 26% performance drop on Deepseek
model, and we also analyse the connection of spectral properties of the subgraphs
to downstream response generation.
INTRODUCTION
Enhancing language models with structured knowledge has gained significant attention, as it enables more context-aware and factually grounded responses (Liu et al., 2020; Sanmartin, 2024),
especially in domains such as healthcare, finance, and law where incorrect reasoning can have serious consequences. Since these agents consist of multiple intertwined components, in such settings
the interpretability and reasoning over structured relationships are indispensable. However, most research has focused on improving retrieval while assuming the integrity of the underlying knowledge
graph (KG), overlooking the vulnerabilities that adversarial perturbations may introduce.
Targeted edits to a KG—such as adding or removing relations—can disrupt retrieval and mislead
downstream generation. Since KG-RAG systems embed retrieved subgraphs (from databases or
text) into prompts, even small perturbations can shift model reasoning. For example, deleting the Diabetes →Hypertension edge in the query “What causes Hypertension?” (Figure 1) hides the causal
link. Our threat model assumes only read access to retrieved subgraphs, often exposed through APIs
or prompt-visible contexts. Yet, no systematic framework exists to test KG-RAG robustness under
such perturbations. Because retrieved triples or text are prompt-injected, adversaries can observe
outputs, reconstruct parts of the KG, and introduce targeted noise via poisoned sources Tan et al.
(2024); Chaudhari et al. (2024).
Motivated by these factors, we propose a novel graph signature framework for black-box vulnerability analysis of KG-RAG systems. Our approach requires only the query-specific subgraph and
is applicable to subgraphs of arbitrary size. By leveraging spectral characteristics, the framework
captures structurally important relations and supports both edge insertions and deletions. To control
perturbation severity, we introduce an attack budget that limits the number of edits per subgraph,
yielding a localized and interpretable corruption mechanism.
To quantify the impact of perturbations, we analyze the graph edit distance as a semantic check.
Empirically, we find that our attacks induce substantial performance degradation while preserving
the overall structural properties of the subgraph. Furthermore, we observe a shift in response length
distributions: unperturbed KGs yield tightly concentrated response lengths, whereas perturbed KGs
produce long-tailed distributions, indicating that models take arbitrarily long to reason and often fail
to reason correctly, consistent with observations in prior work (Dong et al., 2025). Our method takes
significantly lower time than the best baseline, which enhances its practicality.

Our key contributions are as follows. We introduce: (i) a novel graph signature framework that
encodes variable-sized KGs by capturing their spectral properties for systematic vulnerability analysis, (ii) extensive experiments across six widely used benchmarks demonstrating that perturbations
to only 5% of edges can induce performance drops of up to 26% while being significantly faster than
the best baseline with factors of upto 11×. We extend our empirical results to a defense detection
framework highlighting the strength of graph signatures, (iii) detailed quantitative and qualitative
analyses connecting spectral distortions of subgraphs to the behavior of generated responses.
RELATED WORK
KG-RAG Agents: Contrary to the standard RAG agents which enhance language models by retrieving relevant documents in order to improve the factual correctness Lewis et al. (2020); Xu et al.
(2024), KG-RAG strengthens the reasoning by leveraging structured entity-relation graphs Ji et al.
(2021); Huang et al. (2019). This enables multihop inference Panda et al. (2024) and interpretable
reasoning Sanmartin (2024). Recent approaches like MindMap Wen et al. (2023) and context-aware
KG-RAG Panda et al. (2024) are some examples that demonstrate the benefits of structured knowledge retrieval.
Current state of Adversarial Attacks on agentic retrieval frameworks: The RAG agents are
susceptible to several adversarial threats due to the involvement of multiple components - data storing and indexing mechanism (often invoking chunking strategies), retriever that fetches the relevant
context for a given query, reranker which is often used to rerank the retrieved context in order to
select the most important pieces of information, and lastly the generation model. Various attacks
adversarial retrieval attacks have thus been proposed in the literature - Liu et al. (2023); Nazary
et al. (2025); Xue et al. (2024), where crafted inputs mislead or bias the retriever; prompt injection
Greshake et al. (2023), where malicious instructions embedded in input prompts manipulate the
language model’s behavior; data poisoning Zhang et al. (2019); Chaudhari et al. (2024); Tan et al.
(2024), where adversarially modified data degrades or biases downstream responses; and prompt
leakage attacks Liang et al. (2024); Hui et al. (2024); Sternak et al. (2025); Agarwal et al. (2024),
where adversaries extract system prompts—including query conditioned retrieved passages and internal logic via model outputs or probing queries. Of particular importance in this pipeline is the
post-retrieval and pre-generation phase, where the context is retrieved in order to be sent to the
downstream model for generation. This is also the component which is extremely susceptible to
adversarial attacks. Most of the existing approaches however focus on the adversarial attacks over
unstructured retrieval text, overlooking the additional attack surfaces introduced by the structured
nature of knowledge graphs in KG-RAG systems.
KG-RAG attack: The recent work of (Wen et al., 2025) proposed a data poisoning attack, however
this was introduced in order to conduct attacks on the knowledge graph construction process from
raw text. This is a very different setting as compared to our work, where we focus on deployed KGRAG systems and attacking those. To our knowledge, extensive studies on this topic are lacking,
which is one of the primary motivations for this work.
PRELIMINARIES
A Knowledge Graph (KG) is a directed, labeled graph represented as a tuple G = (V, E, R), where
V is the set of entities, R is the set of relation types, and E ⊆V ×R×V is the set of directed edges.
Each edge corresponds to a factual statement in the form of a triple (h, r, t), where h, t ∈V are the
head and tail entities, and r ∈R denotes their relationship. G is also associated with a function
W : E →R+ assigning confidence scores to edges. Additionally, each entity and relation may have
associated features, such as semantic attributes or learned embeddings, which enrich the graph for
downstream tasks such as reasoning, retrieval, and question answering.
A KG-RAG (Knowledge Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation) system integrates a knowledge graph into the retrieval-augmented generation pipeline. Given a query q ∈Q, where Q is a
set of input queries, we define a combined generic retrieval function fret : q →Gq that generates
the query specific subgraph Gq = (Vq, Eq, Rq). This serves as context for the generation process,
where it is passed along with q to a LLM, improving response quality and reducing hallucinations.
We discuss the KG generation process in appendix L.

4.4
BASELINE ATTACKS
As discussed earlier, our work is one of the first to propose a generic attack framework for KG-RAG
and thus a complete characterization is missing in the literature. To this end, we consider two of
the most widely used methods from the literature to generate structural perturbations. Betweenness
Centrality (BC) based attack that target nodes and edges critical to information flow; their removal
disrupts key pathways, fragmenting Gq and degrading retrieval quality. Random Perturbations (RP)
attack that involve uninformed deletions and insertions of nodes and edges. Though less strategic,
they still impact graph connectivity and semantic coherence, serving as a baseline for comparison.

DENSE VS SPARSE SUBGRAPHS
An important characteristic of KGs being used as the context also associate to the size of Gq. We
perform this experiment to note the performance drops by segregating the graphs into two groups.
This is done by first computing the ratio |Eq|

into second (called Sparse). Note the phrasing Dense and Sparse is merely for categorization and
does not quantify any absolute graph property here. The results in figure 8 show a few interesting
trends: (i) the performance for no attack (without attack WA) is higher for dense subgraphs as
compared to sparse ones where reasoning paths are more explicit for the model to condition upon,

attacks as compared to sparse graphs, which highlights that the models rely heavily on the direct
reasoning paths during generation, the lack of which steers the models to arbitrary reasoning chains
and much longer responses which are often incorrect (discussed in next subsection).

5.4
EMPIRICAL EVALUATION OF DEFENSE VIA SPECTRAL DETECTION
To empirically evaluate the feasibility of detecting KGRAG-based attacks, we formulated a binary
classification task to distinguish between clean query-specific subgraphs (Gq) and their perturbed
counterparts (G′
q).
We trained a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier utilizing our Graph
Signature (GS) features—specifically the eigenvalue shifts ∆λe
i—and benchmarked the detection
performance against embeddings derived from state-of-the-art GNN encoders, including RGCN
Schlichtkrull et al. (2018), KGAT Wang et al. (2019), and GAT Veliˇckovi´c et al. (2017).
Our results in table 3 indicate that spectral features provide a significantly more distinct signal for
identifying structural perturbations compared to standard message-passing embeddings. The classifier trained on Graph Signature features achieved superior detection scores (up to 0.83) compared
to the baselines, which struggled to generalize (hovering between 0.49–0.58). This suggests that
while GNNs effectively capture semantic node features, they may smooth over the specific structural fragility exploited by spectral attacks. See Appendix I for the detailed experimental setup.

CONCLUSION
We introduced a spectral attack framework for perturbing query-specific subgraphs in KG-RAG under a black-box setting with only read-only access to the retrieved KG. Experiments across multiple
benchmarks and models show that even minimal perturbations can significantly degrade LLM performance, leading to longer and often incorrect responses. As GraphRAG becomes more widely
adopted, our findings underscore the need to study and address its vulnerabilities. We also discuss
initial defense directions, such as randomized retrieval and redundancy, which directly counter the
mechanisms our attack exploits.

DETAILS ABOUT THE DATASET
For analyzing the capabilities of graph signature in attacking KG RAGs and detecting malicious
KGs, we use diverse QA datasets spanning medical, general domain, and expert knowledge elaborated here:
• PubMedQA Jin et al.: A biomedical question answering dataset containing 1,000 researchpaper-based questions with yes/no/maybe answers, derived from PubMed abstracts. Each
instance includes a question, a context (the abstract), and an answer with supporting evidence.
• MS MARCO Bajaj et al. (2018): A large-scale machine reading comprehension dataset
containing approximately 1 million questions sampled from real Bing search queries, with
human-generated answers and relevant passages from web documents.
• HotpotQA Yang et al. (2018):
A multi-hop question answering dataset with 113K
Wikipedia-based questions requiring reasoning over multiple documents. Distinguished
by its focus on complex reasoning chains and diverse question types.
• ExpertQA Malaviya et al. (2023): A specialized dataset focusing on expert-level questions
across various domains, designed to evaluate deep domain understanding and complex reasoning capabilities.
• EManual Nandy et al. (2021): A technical manual-based QA dataset containing questions
about electronic device operation and troubleshooting, derived from product manuals and
technical documentation.
• DelucionQA Sadat et al. (2023): A dataset specifically designed to evaluate models’ susceptibility to delusional responses, containing adversarially crafted questions and contexts.

EFFECT OF PERTURBATIONS ON GRAPH SIGNATURE
In this section we discuss the effect of different perturbations on the graph signature and also
illustrate how the spectral gaps change when perturbations are made to the graph structure.
The Clean Graph Signature serves as a Baseline Spectral Fingerprint, where the spectral gaps
of the unperturbed-graph-Laplacian capture the original structure of the graph. This unperturbed
state provides a reference against which changes due to structural modifications can be measured.
The Relation Addition Signature is characterized by localized eigenvalue shifts, which appear as
sharp bright spikes in the difference heatmap. The spectral gap undergoes significant changes,
with an increase in algebraic connectivity (λ2), indicating improved overall connectivity of the
graph. Additionally, higher eigenvalues shift upward, signifying faster diffusion and enhanced
clustering properties. The resulting structural modification leads to an increase in both ∥g∥1 and
∥g∥2, suggesting that the graph becomes more structured and exhibits greater coherence.
The Relation Deletion Signature exhibits localized spectral drops, visualized as deep red
patches in the heatmap. The spectral gap undergoes a significant reduction, as λ2 decreases,
signaling a decline in connectivity. Simultaneously, higher eigenvalues shift downward, causing
slower diffusion and reduced clustering efficiency. The widening of spectral gaps is reflected in the
increase of ∥g∥1, highlighting the emerging fragmentation within the graph. The heatmap clearly
captures this structural degradation, making it possible to quantify the extent of damage caused
by relation removal.
These spectral signatures provide a rigorous framework for analyzing structural modifications in
graphs. By systematically interpreting heatmaps, it becomes possible to detect and quantify changes
resulting from entity and relation perturbations, offering valuable insights into the resilience, connectivity, and stability of graph-based systems.

We evaluate our KG-RAG pipeline using the following metrics: (i) Cosine Similarity, (ii) Groundedness, (iii) Relevance, and (iv) Correctness.
Given the golden answer, the KG-RAG pipeline’s generated response, the retrieved facts, and the
