BRIDGING THE GAP BETWEEN THEORY OF MIND AND ACTION IN LLMS
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
While Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate impressive capabilities in Theory of
Mind (ToM) tasks, translating this social understanding into strategic action remains a
critical challenge. In this paper, we address the Knowing Doing Gap in social intelligence, where models correctly infer the mental states of others yet fail to select actions
consistent with those inferences. To rigorously evaluate this discrepancy, we introduce the
Theory of Mind-to-Action (ToMA) benchmark, a unified framework designed to analyze the causal link between social reasoning and decision making in diverse cooperative
and competitive scenarios. Our empirical analysis reveals that reasoning capabilities do
not guarantee strategic behavior, identifying specific failure modes where reasoning decouples from action. To bridge this gap, we propose alignment mechanisms that enforce
a sequential dependency between perspective taking and execution. By prioritizing the
explicit analysis of others’ mental states before decision making, social reasoning strictly
conditions strategic actions.
INTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently achieved striking advances in complex reasoning and
knowledge-intensive tasks OpenAI (2023); Chowdhery et al. (2022); Team (2023); DeepSeek-AI et al.
(2025). Alongside these gains, a growing body of work suggests that LLMs exhibit Theory of Mind (ToM)
Premack & Woodruff (1978); Baron-Cohen et al. (1985) capabilities, defined as the ability to infer the beliefs, intentions, and mental states of others Strachan et al. (2024); Kosinski (2024); Chen et al. (2024);
Marchetti et al. (2023).
Despite these strides, recent studies reveal a critical failure mode known as the Knowing-Doing Gap Schmied
et al. (2025); Paglieri et al. (2024); Ruoss et al. (2024); Wong et al. (2025). Even when a model derives the
correct inference, it often selects an action that contradicts that inference during decision making. This
inconsistency undermines confidence in the reliability of LLM decisions and raises questions about their
robustness, thereby limiting their potential as effective autonomous agents.
We specifically address this issue in the context of ToM, where inferred mental states must directly guide
strategic choices. ToM is more than a passive diagnostic ability; it serves as a prerequisite for strategic action
in social environments. It enables agents to coordinate toward shared goals in cooperative environments and
to anticipate and counter opponents in competitive settings.
However, LLMs frequently fail to translate accurate social inferences (Knowing) into appropriate behavioral
choices (Doing). As illustrated in the one-way communication scenario in Figure 1, Bob often remains
silent despite correctly predicting that Alice awaits a response, or conversely, speaks when predicting she
will not wait. This confirms that the Knowing-Doing Gap extends to social intelligence, revealing a critical
disconnect between mental state reasoning and strategic action.

While prior research Riemer et al. (2025) raised concerns about inconsistencies between ToM reasoning
and action, it relies on toy cases and lacks rigorous validation. Furthermore, as highlighted by Riemer
et al. (2025), mainstream ToM benchmarks focus on attributing mental states or predicting behavior of other
agents, overlooking the agent’s own choices. Consequently, the field lacks a systematic understanding of
whether LLMs leverage social reasoning to guide their actions.
Contributions. To bridge the gap between ToM reasoning and behavior, this paper establishes a framework evaluating how social understanding translates into strategic action. Beyond assessment, we propose
alignment mechanisms that force ToM insights to inform strategic decision making. Our main contributions
follow:
• ToMA Benchmark: We introduce the Theory of Mind-to-Action (ToMA) benchmark designed to quantitatively analyze the causal link between ToM inference and decision making across diverse cooperative
and competitive scenarios.
• Diagnostic Insights: We identify specific failure modes where ToM reasoning is decoupled from action,
offering critical insights into the limitations of current LLMs in social intelligence.
• Methodology for Alignment: We propose ToM-CoT and ToM-Guide to mitigate the Knowing-Doing
Gap by aligning ToM insights with action policies. ToM-CoT enforces perspective-taking within structured reasoning, while ToM-Guide employs a multi-turn pipeline strictly conditioning action on pregenerated analysis.
THEORY OF MIND-TO-ACTION (TOMA)
2.1
OVERVIEW
We introduce the Theory of Mind to Action (ToMA) benchmark, a unified framework evaluating whether
LLMs translate social reasoning into strategic action. Unlike previous benchmarks treating ToM as a standalone classification task, ToMA ensures that solving the task remains strictly contingent on translating ToM
inferences into action, thereby directly evaluating whether the model bridges social reasoning and behavior.
The benchmark comprises four scenarios categorized into cooperative and competitive settings, as well as
single-turn and multi-turn interactions.

2.2
DESIGN PHILOSOPHY & DISTINCTIONS
While existing ToM benchmarks are limited to assessing the presence of social reasoning capabilities, ToMA
shifts the focus to the translation of this reasoning into behavior. We distinguish our approach through four
key design principles:
• Action oriented strategic evaluation: We shift the paradigm from passive mental state attribution (e.g.,
”What does Alice think?”) to active strategic execution (e.g., ”Given what Alice thinks, what will you
do?”). This approach tests the alignment between social understanding and actual strategic behavior.
• Deterministic causal dependency: To ensure a rigorous link between reasoning and behavior, we design
scenarios where correct ToM inference is the necessary and sufficient condition for the unique optimal
action under conditions. This ensures that success reflects alignment rather than coincidental completion.
• Complex multi-agent modeling: ToMA simulates real world social dynamics involving information
asymmetry, strategic utility, and hidden agendas. Agents must synthesize divergent perspectives from
partial observability to navigate interactions, such as coordinating with allies or deceiving adversaries.
2.3
SCENARIO DESCRIPTIONS
ToMA consists of four distinct games, as illustrated in Figure 2, each tailored to evaluate a specific facet of
social reasoning.
• Partially Observable Game (PO): This scenario centers on information asymmetry where selecting
unique destinations others avoid requires the agent to link ToM reasoning directly to behavior. Identifying the optimal action remains impossible unless the agent translates perspective modeling into execution
(Appendix C.1).

• Minority Game (MG): This game involves preference based reasoning where identifying minority options depends strictly on translating social preference analysis into action. Success ensures the agent
converts its understanding of others’ likes into a final decision (Appendix C.2).
• Speak or Wait (SW): This multi-turn game requires the agent to lead allies to the target location via
precise strategic communication while keeping adversaries strictly unaware of the final objective. Achieving the goal depends on applying ToM to avoid communication overlap with teammates and coordinate
optimal timing while evading enemy detection (Appendix C.3).
• Ambiguity Game (AG): This multi-turn game focuses on strategic execution in mixed motive settings.
The agent must evaluate the mental states of both allies and adversaries to provide hints that selectively
guide teammates while misleading enemies (Appendix C.4).

from social inference to behavioral output. Figure 3 illustrates the gap between ToM and action. As Chain

EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we analyze the ToM Knowing-Doing Gap by examining the discrepancy between ToM inference and execution. We then evaluate how effectively ToM-CoT and ToM-Guide bridge this gap across
strategic environments. Our experiments address the following research questions:
RQ1: The ToM Knowing-Doing Gap. To what extent does a discrepancy exist between a model’s ToM
inference and its actual behavioral execution?
RQ2: Strategy Effectiveness. How effectively do our proposed strategies bridge the identified gap to
improve strategic performance?
RQ3: Impact of ToM Complexity. How does the effectiveness of our proposed strategies vary across
different levels of recursive social reasoning?
Models
We evaluate our methods across four distinct strategic scenarios within our proposed ToMA
benchmark. We test various state-of-the-art LLMs to ensure generalizability, including the Qwen-2.5 seriesQwen et al. (2025), Gemma-3 seriesTeam et al. (2025), GPT-4o-miniOpenAI (2023), GPT-5.1OpenAI
(2025a), Gemini-2.0-flashTeam (2023), and Gemini-2.5-flash-liteComanici et al. (2025). For these models,
we operate them in instant mode to measure their direct response capabilities. Additionally, we include
o4-miniOpenAI (2025b), which we execute in thinking mode to assess the impact of native long reasoning
on strategic performance. We run all models five times and average their performance.
Baselines
To benchmark the performance of ToM-CoT and ToM-Guide, we compare them against three
primary baselines: Zero-shot, where the model generates a final action A directly from the context X; CoT,
which employs standard step-by-step reasoning without explicit ToM mandates; and Self-consistency (SC)
Wang et al. (2022), for which we perform 10 independent iterations and determine the final output through
majority voting. For implementation, ToM-CoT enforces a two-stage internal monologue (ToM Reasoning
followed by Action) in a single turn, while ToM-Guide utilizes a two-step pipeline that re-feeds the model’s
own inference as explicit context for the final decision.
4.1
QUANTIFYING THE TOM-ACTION GAP (RQ1)
We first analyze the disconnect between ToM inference and strategic execution by examining two primary
factors: model scale and ToM accuracy. As defined in Section 3.1, LLMs often lack the capacity to translate
ToM insights into corresponding actions, a phenomenon we quantify through Action Consistency (Cact).
Figure 5a presents Cact for the PO and MG environments, while Figure 7a illustrates results for the SW
game. Across these environments, we observe that consistency remains notably low, staying below 60% for
most models with the exceptions of Qwen-2.5-14B and 32B.

Impact of Model Scale
When examining the impact of model scale, we find that larger models generally
exhibit higher alignment between ToM and action. In PO, MG, and SW, Cact consistently improves as the
parameter count increases. This trend suggests that as underlying reasoning performance matures in larger
models, their capacity to maintain consistency between internal social inferences and behavioral outputs also
strengthens.
Influence of ToM Accuracy
We investigate how social reasoning accuracy affects performance via a
ground truth (GT) injection experiment (Figure 5b). In this setup, we provide ground truth ToM values
as input to evaluate whether the model follows the correct social reasoning path. Ideally, seamless social
knowledge translation should yield a 1.0 success rate at zero inference error. Even with perfect information,
action accuracy fails to reach this threshold. This shortfall proves ToM inferences do not fully govern
decision making, highlighting a disconnect in the knowing doing link.
Nevertheless, we observe a correlation between action accuracy and ToM accuracy. Declining action performance as GT error rates increase suggests that injecting ToM results into inputs influences model behavior,
although this influence remains partial. Furthermore, compared to the baseline, providing explicit social
inferences narrows the reasoning action gap when the GT error rate falls below 0.7, corresponding to ToM
accuracy above 0.3. These findings imply that strategies like ToM Guide help models anchor decisions in
social reasoning. Therefore, when a model has sufficient ToM accuracy, providing explicit social inferences
links internal knowledge to strategic decisions.

4.3
SENSITIVITY TO REASONING LEVELS (RQ3)
We evaluate our strategies across various levels of recursive ToM reasoning, from non-social tasks to complex higher-order inferences. Figure 7b illustrates the performance breakdown by ToM level.
In Level 0 (L0) scenarios, where success depends on maximizing individual utility without considering others, we find that the Zero shot baseline achieves highest accuracy. In this context, our strategies underperform
because forcing social reasoning introduces unnecessary complexity to simple optimization problems.
However, as tasks demand recursive Theory of Mind (Level 1 and above), this trend reverses. While the
CoT baseline outperforms Zero shot by introducing reasoning steps, it consistently falls short of our methods.
Notably, as the ToM level increases towards Level 4, representing a fully observable and complex setting, the
performance gap between our strategies and baselines widens. This implies that while standard prompting
methods struggle with high order social dynamics, our approach successfully bridges the knowing doing
gap. Thus, connecting ToM to action becomes vital as strategic complexity increases, and our approach
effectively maintains this connection.
RELATED WORKS
ToM in LLMs and Benchmarks
Theory of Mind refers to the ability to attribute beliefs, intentions,
and informational constraints to others (Premack & Woodruff, 1978; Baron-Cohen et al., 1985). Early
evaluations of LLMs often relied on adaptations of the classic Sally-Anne false-belief test to probe whether
models could attribute mental states (Kosinski, 2024; Strachan et al., 2024). To enable more systematic
assessment, researchers have developed specialized benchmarks that extend beyond these simple diagnostic
tasks. Le et al. (2019) introduced ToMi, utilizing simulated stories to test latent belief tracking. Building on
this foundation, OpenToM (Xu et al., 2024) and FANToM (Kim et al., 2023) assess ToM in more complex
narratives and conversational settings characterized by information asymmetry. Furthermore, Hi-ToM (He
et al., 2023) explicitly targets higher-order recursive reasoning capabilities. Most recently, ToMBench (Chen
et al., 2024) offers a unified framework to evaluate these diverse social reasoning skills. However, these
benchmarks predominantly treat ToM as a passive diagnostic capability. They evaluate whether a model
infers what an agent believes but do not test whether the model uses these inferred mental states to select
actions.

The Knowing-Doing Gap in Reasoning
Beyond social reasoning, recent studies focus on a broad
Knowing-Doing Gap where models articulate rationales yet fail to act. Turpin et al. (2023) reveal that
generated chain-of-thought explanations often misrepresent true decision making, proving that reasoning
does not guarantee execution. Similarly, Pal et al. (2025) highlight in Incoherent Beliefs & Inconsistent
Actions that models harbor correct latent knowledge but contradict it during decision making. This disconnect extends to ethical alignment; Shen et al. (2025) demonstrate in Mind the Value-Action Gap that LLMs
claiming specific values frequently violate them in practice. Furthermore, Schmied et al. (2025) argue in
LLMs are Greedy Agents that reinforcement learning fine tuning biases models toward greedy policies that
override deliberative reasoning.
The ToM Knowing-Doing Gap
Despite these insights, relatively few studies directly examine the translation of ToM inferences into behavioral choices. Notably, Riemer et al. (2025) argue that existing benchmarks
primarily assess the mere possession of ToM capabilities rather than their connection to behavior, highlighting a functional gap where social knowledge fails to guide decision-making. Furthermore, while multi-agent
games like Werewolf or Diplomacy implicitly require social reasoning (Xu et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023),
they are unsuitable for evaluating this specific mechanism. In such complex environments, outcomes depend
on multifaceted factors, making it difficult to determine whether an action resulted strictly from ToM reasoning or other confounding variables. Therefore, to rigorously assess whether ToM translates into action, we
require a dedicated benchmark where the optimal choice depends exclusively on accurate social inference.
CONCLUSION
In this paper, we address the ToM Knowing-Doing Gap where LLMs fail to translate Theory of Mind capabilities into strategic actions. To quantify this disconnect, we introduce the Theory of Mind-to-Action (ToMA)
benchmark. Analysis reveals that reasoning capabilities do not guarantee strategic behavior, as models frequently decouple actions from their own social inferences. To bridge this gap, we propose ToM-CoT and
ToM-Guide, enforcing a structured dependency between perspective-taking and execution to ensure that every social insight translates directly into a tactical choice. These mechanisms align internal knowledge with
behavioral policies, proving that social intelligence requires explicitly linking reasoning to action.

A
LIMITATIONS
While our work offers significant insights into the transition from social reasoning to action, we acknowledge
several limitations that future research should address.
First, although ToMA encompasses diverse social dynamics, the scenarios remain simplified abstractions of
real-world interactions. In natural social settings, agents must process multimodal cues and navigate openended contexts where the optimal action is not always deterministic. Consequently, our benchmark might
not fully capture the nuance of human-like social adaptability in unstructured environments.
Second, our proposed alignment strategies impose additional computational costs. ToM-Guide, in particular, requires a multi-step inference pipeline that generates explicit social reasoning before determining an
action. This sequential process increases inference latency and token consumption compared to standard
zero-shot prompting. Future work could explore more efficient methods, such as fine-tuning models on
ToM-action pairs to internalize this alignment without the need for inference-time intervention.
Finally, our methods rely heavily on the underlying reasoning capability of the model. As observed in our
experiments, alignment strategies yield minimal gains when the model lacks fundamental Theory of Mind
accuracy. This dependency suggests that while we can bridge the gap between knowing and doing, we
cannot compensate for a fundamental lack of social understanding.

Dataset Generation and Content Safety
The scenarios in the ToMA benchmark were constructed using
a combination of human design and LLM assistance (e.g., GPT-4). We conducted a manual review of the

LLM-generated text can occasionally produce unexpected outputs, and we encourage future research to
maintain vigilance when employing similar data generation strategies.
Potential Biases
Our benchmark aims to cover diverse social dynamics, including cooperation and competition. Nevertheless, the scenarios may reflect the implicit biases of the authors or the underlying models
used during construction. We recognize that social norms vary across cultures, and the ”optimal” actions
defined in our benchmark may not universally apply to all cultural contexts.
Risks of Strategic Agents
Finally, ToMA evaluates the ability of agents to take strategic actions, which
includes deception in competitive settings (e.g., Minority Game). While our focus is on evaluating reasoning capabilities in controlled environments, we emphasize the importance of monitoring these behaviors to
prevent potential misuse in real-world applications where honesty and safety are paramount.

resolved only when combined with the Teammate’s private knowledge.
