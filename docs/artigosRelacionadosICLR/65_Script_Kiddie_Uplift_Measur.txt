SCRIPT KIDDIE UPLIFT: MEASURING PROCEDURAL
MISUSE AMPLIFICATION IN AI AGENTS
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
We study diffuse cybersecurity risk and how AI agent-generated code uplifts
novice attackers.
We focus on script kiddies—low-skill opportunistic attackers who stand to gain the most from AI-assisted coding—and examine procedural misuse amplification: whether models assist harmful goals when requests
are decomposed into benign substeps. We develop a benchmark of eight cyberoffensive tasks spanning five MITRE ATT&CK tactics, designed to be lowresource, novice-accessible, and reflective of exploits a script kiddie might realistically attempt. Each task is decomposed into substeps validated by experts in
feasibility of completion. Evaluating 10 models across four families, we find three
key results. First, step-by-step interactions rarely trigger refusal, even when the
same models recognize safety concerns in separate evaluation. Second, most models produce functionally-complete code, with Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5, and GPT-5
Mini providing the most operationally-ready outputs. Third, in a human study
with 39 novice participants, the code group completed more steps in seven of eight
tasks compared to an internet-only baseline. Among participants who completed
tasks in both settings, weaker performers benefited disproportionately—those below the 60th percentile closed on average 37% of the gap to top performers. These
findings demonstrate that multi-step decomposition circumvents current safety
measures and that LLMs measurably lower barriers for precisely the population
least capable without them.
INTRODUCTION
As frontier models become capable coding agents and partners, understanding their potential to enable cybersecurity harms is critical for AI safety. Real-world cyberattacks are inherently multi-step
processes with many dual-use components: reconnaissance scripts, credential handling, payload delivery, and persistence mechanisms can each serve legitimate purposes in isolation. Unlike robustness evaluations focused on refusal of overtly harmful requests, determining whether a piece of code
enables harm is often contextual—and attackers need not reveal malicious intent within individual
substeps to extract useful assistance from language models. This creates a fundamental challenge
for deployed safety measures: models may refuse explicitly harmful requests while still providing
components that, when assembled, enable the same harmful outcomes. We term this phenomenon
procedural misuse amplification.
Recent evidence suggests this risk is not hypothetical. A Reuters investigation found that major
AI chatbots readily assisted in crafting phishing campaigns targeting elderly victims, with most
models complying after minimal prompt modification (Reuters, 2025). Phishing—often requiring
little technical sophistication—is the most reported cybercrime in the United States, with Americans
over 60 losing at least $4.9 billion to online fraud in 2024 alone (Federal Bureau of Investigation,
2025). Meanwhile, last year CISA has warned that unsophisticated actors using basic intrusion
techniques are increasingly targeting critical infrastructure, causing significant disruption despite
their limited skill (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, 2025). These developments
underscore that diffuse, low-skill threats may pose greater aggregate risk.
We focus on this threat model: script kiddies—novice attackers who perform opportunistic exploits
using available tools and softwares that they have limited understanding of (Mead & Stehney, 2005).
Traditionally treated as a secondary threat actor (Chng et al., 2022), this population is especially relSubmitted to ICLR 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild. Do not distribute.
evant to study in the presence of large language models. LLMs provide broad procedural guidance,
generate functional code on demand, and can systematically lower the barrier to executing multi-step
attacks—precisely the capabilities that script kiddies lack.
To evaluate these risks, we develop a benchmark of eight cyber-offensive tasks grounded in our
threat model, spanning five categories of MITRE ATT&CK Tactics (The MITRE Corporation,
2025). Tasks include phishing website creation, denial of service attacks, deepfake generation, dark
web scraping, and supply chain exploitation—selected to be low-resource, require only novice-level
skill, and reflect realistic opportunistic attacks. Each task is decomposed into substeps simulating
how an attacker might prompt a language model for assistance.
Using this benchmark, we investigate three questions:
1. Do current models demonstrate situational awareness of accumulating risk during multistep conversations progressing toward cyber-offensive goals?
2. How operationally ready is the code that models provide for dual-use cybersecurity tasks?
3. How substantial is the uplift for novice attackers given LLM-generated code, compared to
a baseline with internet access alone?
Our work makes three contributions: (1) a task suite of multi-step cyber-offensive tasks grounded in
a realistic threat model, (2) an evaluation framework that separately measures safety awareness, refusal behavior, and code quality (3) empirical evidence from automated evaluation and a controlled
human study demonstrating that LLM assistance is meaningfully helpful and can aid low-skill attackers. Together, these findings highlight a gap in current safety measures and motivate defenses
that account for procedural, contextually harmful interactions.
RELATED WORK
2.1
AI-ENABLED CYBER THREATS
Various technology companies have posted threat reports where they describe they identified AIenabled cyberattacks that leveraged their AI models (Moix et al., 2025). For instance, Anthropic
published a report on how they detected an “AI-orchestrated espionage campaign” lead by a nation
state (Anthropic, 2025). Similarly, Google and OpenAI have created reports where they describe
how different actors misused their chatbots (Google, 2025; Nimmo et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2025).
These companies have incorporated various safeguards to avoid their models from showing inappropriate or harmful responses to users (Anthropic, 2025; Google, 2025; OpenAI, 2025; Meta, 2026).
However, researchers and journalists have found that LLMs were complying and successful in writing phishing emails to scam the elderly (Reuters, 2025). Moreover, cybercriminals have created
their own LLMs Lin et al. (2024).
2.2
LLM CYBERSECURITY CAPABILITY EVALUATIONS
A growing body of work evaluates the cybersecurity capabilities of large language models. Phuong
et al. (2024) conduct dangerous capability evaluations that include cybersecurity-related tasks. Wan
et al. (2024) measure LLM offensive security capabilities across automated social engineering, manual cyber operations, and autonomous attacks. In their uplift experiment using capture-the-flag simulations, they find negligible benefit from Llama 3 assistance for both novices and experts. However,
their novice population consist of technical employees at Meta without cybersecurity backgrounds,
and LLM assistance is constrained to a single model. Our work targets a lower-skill population, uses
tasks designed for opportunistic attackers, and do not restrict to one model.
Several benchmarks focus on autonomous agent capabilities. Rein et al. (2025) introduce digitally
realizable tasks and evaluate agent success under varying scaffolding conditions against human baselines. Zhu et al. (2025) benchmark agents on exploiting real-world web application vulnerabilities.
Kuntz et al. (2025) introduce OS-Harm, a benchmark using realistic operating system tools to study
both harm potential and robustness of computer-use agents. Yuan et al. (2024) benchmark LLMs’
proficiency in judging safety risks from agent interaction records, finding substantial room for improvement even in frontier models. Our work differs in focus: rather than measuring autonomous

2.3
PROCEDURAL MISUSE AND TASK DECOMPOSITION
A growing body of work demonstrates that task decomposition undermines current safety measures. Jones et al. (2025) show that adversaries can misuse combinations of individually safe models
by decomposing tasks into subtasks, solving challenging-but-benign subtasks with aligned frontier
models and easy-but-malicious subtasks with weaker models. Yueh-Han et al. (2025) study the related phenomenon of sequentially contextual harm, finding that task decomposition substantially
reduces refusal rates and that frontier models used as monitors struggle to detect malicious intent
from decomposed subtasks. Our work demonstrates this phenomenon in a realistic setting: we apply
decomposition to cyber-offensive tasks grounded in the script kiddie threat model and validate uplift
through a controlled human study.
2.4
CAPABILITY EVALUATIONS AND THREAT MODELING
Lukoˇsi¯ut˙e & Swanda (2025) argue that capability focused evaluations for cybersecurity does not
inform real world threat due to the lack of evaluation from a risk assessment frame that includes
analysis of threat actor adoption behavior and potential for impact such as economic impact. They
recommend more realistic modeling of attacker behavior, which our work complements.
2.5
SCRIPT KIDDIE
The term script kiddie emerged in 1990s hacker forums to describe novices who exploit systems
without deep understanding of their tools (Mead & Stehney, 2005). Studies characterize their motivations as curiosity, recognition-seeking, and boredom (McDermott & Fox, 1999; Chng et al.,
2022). While automation tools like phishing kits have lowered some barriers, server-side attacks remain challenging for novices (Cova et al., 2008; Holm & Sommestad, 2017); recent work explores
whether LLMs change this calculus (Moskal et al., 2023).
Despite academic and industry focus on sophisticated adversaries like nation-states (Dingledine
et al., 2004; Trellix Advanced Research Center, 2025), prior work has used novice threat models
to demonstrate attack feasibility (Ntantogian et al., 2015; Roberts et al., 2023), and evidence suggests low-skill attacks remain pervasive with substantial impact (Staff, 2007; Verizon Enterprise
Solutions, 2025).
METHOD
3.1
THREAT MODEL
We focus on the following threat model:
• Adversary: script kiddie.
• Motivations: Opportunistic and attention-seeking; gain reputation by demonstrating successful system compromise or misuse.
• Capabilities: Low; beginner-level coding experience and familiarity with using software
applications.
• Resources: Low; relies on readily available and low cost software, services, and tools such
as large language models.
• Targets: Flexible; attacks are more likely to be opportunity-driven rather than focused on
a fixed target.

3.3
LLM RESPONSE EVALUATION
3.3.1
TASK SETUP
We evaluate model behavior on a suite of multi-step dual-use cybersecurity tasks. Each task is
decomposed into a fixed sequence of substeps, where individual steps may be benign in isolation
but form a harmful procedure when composed. All tasks are standardized with a predefined set of
steps, each described by a short sentence specifying the expected outcome of that step.
For all automated evaluations, we run tasks as multi-turn conversations, with each turn beginning
with “How can I” to mimic a user query. Model responses are evaluated using the procedures
described below. Full prompts and criteria are provided in section B.
3.3.2
REFUSAL EVALUATION
At each task substep, an automated judge classifies the model’s response into one of three categories:
Refusal (no actionable code or instructions), Soft Refusal (safety or refusal disclaimers accompanied
by actionable code or instructions), and Action (actionable assistance without disclaimers). Classification is performed independently per substep based solely on the model’s response.

3.3.3
SAFETY AWARENESS EVALUATION
Separately from refusal behavior, we evaluate whether models express awareness of potential safety
risks in a conversation. At each task substep, we perform an additional inference pass that asks the
same model to assess the safety concern of the conversation so far.
This query includes the full conversation history up to the current step, excluding the model’s most
recent response. The model reports one of four concern levels: None, Low, Medium, or High.
This safety awareness assessment is independent of the main multi-step code generation and does
not influence the model’s response.
3.3.4
CODE QUALITY EVALUATION
We further evaluate the quality and operational usefulness of code provided in non-refusal responses,
focusing on whether outputs are actionable in practice rather than merely descriptive. Our goal is to
assess the extent to which responses meaningfully lower the barrier to execution for dual-use cyber
tasks.
We introduce a structured code quality rubric applied at the level of individual task substeps. Each
substep is scored along nine dimensions on a 0–3 scale:
1. Executability: whether the provided code or commands can be run in their current form.
2. Operational steps: the presence of concrete setup, execution, and verification instructions.
3. Environment realism: whether execution assumptions (e.g., OS, hardware, permissions,
network access) are explicit and plausible.
4. Dependency validity: whether referenced libraries, tools, or APIs are real and correctly
specified.
5. Acquisition and logistics: clarity around required external assets such as datasets, models,
credentials, or API keys.
6. Verification and testability: whether the response provides reproducible tests or demonstrations with clear success criteria.
7. Robustness: handling of failure modes and edge cases, including error messages and retries.
8. Integration: whether components are meaningfully connected into a coherent pipeline.
9. Functional completeness: whether the intended behavior is fully implemented rather than
sketched or stubbed.
For each substep, the judge model produces a structured JSON assessment containing per-dimension
scores, a list of missing or underspecified components, and a brief rationale. This rubric allows us to
distinguish responses that merely outline an approach from those that provide executable, realistic,
and verifiable guidance. This allows us to distinguish conceptual sketches from code a novice could
run without modification.
3.4
HUMAN UPLIFT STUDY
We conduct a human study with novice participants who have beginner-level programming experience. For each task, we select the model producing the highest-quality code as measured by our
automated grader (section 3.3.4), and prompt it to assemble outputs into standalone files.
We evaluate two conditions: no code, in which participants have internet access only, and code, in
which participants additionally receive LLM-generated starter code. For each task, we randomly
assign participants per setting. Participants have 4 hours to complete as many steps as possible.
We ensure all activities remain legal, ethical, and non-harmful through sandboxed environments and
safety guardrails. Full study procedures are described in section D.

4.1
FEW MODELS EXHIBIT ALIGNMENT BETWEEN SAFETY AWARENESS AND RESPONSE
CAUTION
We evaluate 10 models across four model families released between 2024 and 2025. For each task,
we perform inference as a multi-turn conversation following the procedure described in section 3.3.1.
At each task substep, we record the model’s reported safety concern level as defined in section 3.3.3.
Safety awareness is evaluated in a separate inference pass that does not influence the model’s response.
We plot the heatmap of safety awareness by category, and response type in fig. 1. We note that

conversation at the step is judged as moderate or high concern in the other inference call. Similarly,

Maverick provide code without disclaimer most of the time, yet in the safety awareness evaluation

such sensitivity does not correlate with refusal behaviors, indicating a lack of alignment between
safety awareness and response caution.

4.2
FUNCTIONAL COMPLETENESS IS COMMON, BUT OPERATIONAL READINESS IS LIMITED
TO A FEW MODELS
Using the code quality rubric defined in section 3.3.4, we evaluate the operational usefulness of
non-refusal responses across all task substeps. We aggregate scores across steps to compute average
code quality for each model.
Overall, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5, and GPT-5 Mini provide the most complete and ready-to-use code
across the evaluated tasks. The most commonly missing components identified by the autograder are
entrypoint commands, error handling, installation instructions, and verification steps. We found that
most models has medium to high score of functional completeness, with only smaller models like
GPT-OSS 20B and Claude 3 Haiku having a functional completeness score of less than 1.5 (fig. 2).

4.3.2
WEAKER NOVICES BENEFIT MORE FROM CODING LLM UPLIFT
We analyze the uplift provided by LLM code further by comparing participant ranking across different settings. Across the 8 tasks, we have 39 unique participants. We calculate score of participant
based on a given task by assigning a score of 1 for every step completed, and a score of 0.5 for every
step partially completed. We tiebreak on the same score based on which submission got further in
steps, then which step completion was completed more quickly if the last completed was the same
step. We normalize the ranking over all valid submissions for the given task. With the rankings we
generate average percentile for each experimental setting of each participant.
We perform analysis based on two filters: participants who have completed at least one valid submission per setting (n=15, min_one_each), and participants that have completed at least 2
valid submissions per setting (n=7, min_two_each). When considering absolute gain conditioned on no code performance for participants that have completed at least one in each setting
(min_one_each), we observe a negative correlation of −0.86 with their no code average percentile, with an R2 of 0.55 (fig. 4). To test robustness against single-trial noise, we repeat the analysis with filtering only for participants with at least two submissions per setting (n=7) and observe
consistent negative correlation of −0.58, with an R2 of 0.42 for min_two_each (fig. 9). We find
that despite individual variation and task differences, there is a negative relationship between the absolute rank changes, and the participants’ no code task performance percentiles. Weaker participants
get greater gains from the availability of LLM code.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
We study procedural misuse amplification using a small, curated set of tasks with minimal environments. Future work can expand the task set and increase task sophistication to better characterize
models’ safety awareness and identify which substeps are most critical for enabling harmful outcomes.
For the uplift study, we provide fixed LLM-generated code for each task to maintain experimental
control and to approximate a low-sophistication attacker who relies on direct, multi-turn assistance.
Given that most models rarely refuse and often produce functionally complete code in multi-turn
ask, we use a human study to validate whether this assistance is practically helpful. Our study does
not target worst-case uplift; future work could examine richer co-working settings between humans
and agents without fixing the code.
Lastly, uplift is evaluated in a research setting where participants are aware of observation and
screen recording, introducing potential demand effects. Participants also know which experimental
condition they are in. Due to resource constraints, we cap attempts at four hours, which may not
fully capture participants’ learning curves as they engage with the tasks.

IMPACT STATEMENT
This paper studies the uplift potential that comes with the availability LLM code, and the misuse
potential for tasks that have dual-use components. Our finding that weaker novices benefit disproportionately from LLM assistance supports capability democratization concerns and we hope informs
safety measures that account for multi-step, contextually harmful interactions. We acknowledge
this research is itself dual-use; to mitigate potential harms, we do not release solutions publicly,
conducted our human study in sandboxed environments preventing real-world harm, and closely
monitored human study progress.
While our work focuses on the script kiddies attacker model, the underlying phenomenon likely extends to other domains and attacker profiles where multi-step processes decompose into individually
benign components, and we encourage future research in areas such as biosecurity and disinformation with appropriate ethical safeguards.

D
DETAILS ON THE NOVICE UPLIFT STUDY
D.1
ENVIRONMENT
Task environments varied based on resource requirements. For two tasks involving deepfake generation, we provisioned cloud GPUs via vast.ai with a GeForce RTX 4090 at a cost of $0.24–$0.40
per hour. GPUs were assigned when participants were ready to begin and automatically shut down
after 4 hours to enforce the time limit.
Several tasks used Docker containers to provide sandboxed, locally-hosted environments. For example, the exposed bucket scraping task required participants to run a containerized web application
and scrape bucket endpoints from the local instance rather than interacting with live cloud infrastructure. This ensured participant activities remained legal and non-harmful while preserving task
realism.

Submitted to ICLR 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild. Do not distribute.
For tasks without GPU or Docker requirements, participants used their own machines with standard
internet access.
D.2
SETUP AND SUBMISSION REQUIREMENTS
Each task began with task instructions and any required configuration files. In the code condition,
the LLM-generated code was provided as part of the instructions. Participants were onboarded after
reviewing a tasking protocol document and safety disclaimers.
Participants were required to:
• Create a private GitHub repository following a specified naming format and grant project
researcher access
• After initial commit that starts the timer, push exactly one commit per step, with commit
messages following a standard format indicating step number and completion status
• Record their full screen throughout the session
• Take a screenshot of output and/or artifact upon completing each step
• Upload a completed survey upon finishing
Participants had 4 hours to complete as many steps as possible. Set up time (such as connecting to
GPU) is excluded from the task time.
D.3
PARTICIPANT RECRUITMENT AND PAYMENT
We recruited college students with one or two years of computer science coursework as our novice
study participants. We recruited over 50 participants and off-boarded any participant that violated
submission guideline, used external AI help, or in other ways demonstrated bad faith in tasking. All
novice participants were paid $50 per hour for their participation, which is higher than the minimum
wage in their country and state of residence.
We recruited four professional programmers with relevant experience to produce reference solutions
in validating that the tasks are feasible, and the sub-steps are clear. The experts were compensated
more than $100 per hour, above the minimum wage in their country and state of residence. Depending on the specific task, some experts aided in task environment design and were compensated
more.
All participants acknowledged the safety disclaimer and confirmed that they would only work within
the defined safe scope of each task.
D.4
EXCLUSION CRITERIA
We excluded submissions that met any of the following criteria:
• Failed to share full screen or record the entire session
• Did not upload screenshots for all completed steps
• Showed usage of LLM assistance in screen recording
• Exceeded the 4-hour time limit
• Demonstrated bad-faith participation (e.g., excessive stalling, completing unrelated tasks,
working on multiple tasks simultaneously)
D.5
QUALITY CONTROL
For each submission, we verified that: (1) the task took 4 hours or less, (2) the GitHub repository and
commit messages followed the required format, (3) the provided GPU or Docker environment was
used when applicable, (4) no external AI assistance beyond web search and provided materials was
used, (5) valid recording matching the submission length exists for quality checking and verification
of no external AI usage. We exclude submissions that do not meet our quality control standards.

Submitted to ICLR 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild. Do not distribute.
D.6
ASSESSMENT OF COMPLETION
Steps were marked as complete if participants produced the expected output and accomplishes the
goal of step. Partial completion credit was assigned for submissions with minor errors such as
schema mismatches, typos, or format differences (e.g., returning a list instead of JSON). We use the
reference solution of each task to aid in grading of the steps.
