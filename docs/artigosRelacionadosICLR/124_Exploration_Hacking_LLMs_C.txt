EXPLORATION HACKING: LLMS CAN LEARN TO
RESIST RL TRAINING
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
Reinforcement learning (RL) has become essential to the reasoning and alignment
post-training of large language models (LLMs). However, successful RL relies on
sufficient exploration of diverse actions by the model during training. We study
whether RL is robust to exploration hacking, where a model strategically alters its
exploration during training to influence the subsequent training outcome. First, we
create model organisms of exploration hacking by using fine-tuning-based “locking”
techniques; we show that these models can successfully resist RL-based capability
elicitation in AI R&D and agentic biosecurity environments, while maintaining
performance on closely related tasks. Next, we use our model organisms to evaluate
the effectiveness of monitoring techniques as detection methods for exploration
hacking. Finally, we show that current frontier models can reason effectively about
suppressing their exploration when presented with simulated RL environments
and encouraged to act strategically. Together, our results empirically establish
exploration hacking as a failure mode of RL on sufficiently capable LLMs.
INTRODUCTION
RL is central to frontier reasoning capabilities, safety training, and dangerous capability assessments
in LLMs. RL with verifiable rewards (Su et al., 2025, RLVR) is used for training reasoning models and
RL from human feedback (RLHF, Ouyang et al., 2022) and AI feedback (RLAIF, Lee et al., 2024)
are established approaches for alignment and behavior shaping. As models cross sensitive capability
thresholds (Anthropic, 2025a; Frontier Model Forum, 2025; OpenAI, 2025a), frontier labs are using
RL-based dangerous capability elicitation to estimate risks before releasing new models (Wallace
et al., 2025). Crucially, developers rely on RL working as expected: developing intended behaviors
and eliciting maximum capability, e.g. so that capability estimates can be trusted (Hubinger, 2023)
and safety cases can rely on techniques like RL-based debate (Irving et al., 2018; Buhl et al., 2025).
However, RL methods are fundamentally dependent on sufficient exploration of diverse actions and
trajectories so that they can be subsequently reinforced. Unlike supervised learning, where training
data is fixed, in RL the model generates its own training data through exploration. Consequently, if a
model were to alter its exploration behavior, the training outcome could be significantly compromised.
This failure mode is known as exploration hacking (Greenblatt, 2025; Shlegeris & Stastny, 2025): an
agent strategically altering its exploration during RL training to influence the training outcome.
While exploration hacking has been hypothesized as a potential failure mode of advanced AI agents,
it has not been empirically validated. As safety cases increasingly depend on RL, stress-testing RL’s
robustness to such failures is critical for AGI safety and security (Google DeepMind, 2025a, Section
6.7). Recent evidence shows that frontier models are beginning to possess increased situational
awareness and precursors of abilities to strategically underperform or fake alignment (Greenblatt
et al., 2024a; Laine et al., 2024), making it plausible that future misaligned models could intentionally
undermine RL training. This motivates work on measures to understand and reduce the threat of
exploration hacking. Indeed, organizations including the UK AI Security Institute have recently
written about the importance of mitigating exploration hacking (Buhl et al., 2025).

Contributions. We introduce exploration hacking as an empirical research problem and make three
main contributions:
1. Establishing the problem. We formalize and decompose exploration hacking (Section 2), and
evaluate frontier models for their propensity and capability to reason strategically about exploration
hacking. In Section 5, we find that current frontier models can exhibit explicit reasoning about
suppressing exploration when presented with simulated RL environments and encouraged to act
strategically.
2. Constructing model organisms. We create the first model organisms (Hubinger et al., 2023)
demonstrating successful selective RL resistance, by using a fine-tuning-based “locking” procedure. In Section 3, we demonstrate that our model organisms can resist capability elicitation
on biosecurity tasks (WMDP, VCT) and AI R&D tasks (BigCodeBench, KernelBench) while
retaining performance on unrelated tasks. We publicly release our model weights and code.
3. Evaluating countermeasures. Using our model organisms, we assess how easily exploration
hacking can be detected. In Section 4, we find that chain-of-thought monitors, action-only monitors
and weight noising can detect our exploration-hackers.
1.1
RELATED WORK
While ensuring sufficient exploration is a classic problem in RL (Sutton & Barto, 2018), the threat
of an agent strategically manipulating its exploration to shape the training outcome and resist RL
training has not been empirically validated. Conceptually, the general idea of models subverting their
own training is known as “gradient hacking” (Hubinger, 2019). More recently, exploration hacking
has been framed as a concrete threat to the safety of modern frontier models (Shlegeris & Stastny,
2025; Greenblatt, 2025), with specific concerns raised about its potential to undermine dangerous
capability elicitation (Hubinger, 2023) and safety cases that rely on techniques like debate (Irving
et al., 2018; Buhl et al., 2025). The rising risk of such behavior is driven by the emergence of
situational awareness (Laine et al., 2024) and strategic reasoning in frontier models, capabilities that
have been demonstrated in recent work on alignment faking (Greenblatt et al., 2024a).
The most direct empirical relatives for our work are studies on “sleeper agents” (Hubinger et al.,
2024), “password-locked models” (Greenblatt et al., 2024b) and “alignment faking” (Greenblatt et al.,
2024a). The first two works both used SFT to create model organisms that only reveal a capability
when a cue is present, and then attempted to suppress (“sleeper agents”) or elicit (“password-locked
models”) it in the relevant case using RL, but were broadly unsuccessful at doing so in the elicitation
setting which we study. We push past this, creating model organisms that resist direct RL elicitation
of a suppressed target capability by selectively under-exploring, evaluating countermeasures, and
auditing frontier models for exploration hacking reasoning. Greenblatt et al. (2024a) study a specific
related behavior; see Appendix A for a discussion of how this and other behaviors relate to exploration
hacking.

CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
Definition. Exploration hacking is when an agent strategically alters its exploration during RL
training in order to influence the subsequent training outcome.
We provide a formal treatment in Appendix A.
Capability decomposition. We decompose exploration hacking into three components that can be
studied separately.
(1) Situational awareness. The model recognizes it is an LLM undergoing RL training, that its
explored outputs are scored, and that these scores drive gradient updates shaping its future policy.
(2) Strategic reasoning. The model reasons about its actions based on its current setting—whether
in deployment, under monitoring, during evaluations, or in training—and acts on this reasoning.
This encompasses the disposition to consider how current actions affect future outcomes, the
ability to derive effective strategies by connecting knowledge about the training process (”my
exploration shapes my updates”) with task understanding (”these actions yield high reward”),
and the capacity to translate such reasoning into coherent, consistently executed behavior.
(3) RL resistance. The model maintains its strategic behavior throughout RL training and successfully shapes the training outcome as planned. For example, resisting capability elicitation
by avoiding high-reward actions, or reinforcing desired values by selectively pairing preferred
behaviors with high rewards actions.
Threat models. We identify three main settings: (i) dangerous capability elicitation, where RL aims
to surface latent harmful capabilities for risk assessment; (ii) beneficial capability elicitation and
development, where RL aims to surface or develop useful capabilities such as AI safety research; and
(iii) alignment training, where RL aims to instill desired behaviors into the model. In all three cases,
a model that strategically explores can prevent RL from achieving its intended outcome—whether
that is accurately estimating worst-case capabilities, developing helpful skills, or reliably aligning
behavior. In this work, we focus on the two elicitation settings:
Dangerous capability elicitation. As models approach sensitive capability thresholds (Anthropic,
2025a; Frontier Model Forum, 2025), accurate red-teaming becomes critical for risk estimation.
Prompt-based evaluation may fail to elicit worst-case capabilities, particularly for open-weight
models vulnerable to malicious fine-tuning (Wallace et al., 2025; Casper et al., 2024). This motivates
RL-based capability elicitation: if dangerous capabilities cannot be surfaced even after RL training, a
stronger safety case can be made. However, such techniques are vulnerable to exploration hacking.
We study this setting by simulating the agentic biosecurity elicitation setup of Wallace et al. (2025).
Beneficial capability elicitation. A model may possess useful capabilities—such as AI (Safety)
R&D (Google DeepMind, 2025b), or reliable monitoring capabilities—that it does not want developers to access. Exploration hacking could cause such models to subtly underperform on tasks that
do not further their goals, even under RL pressure. We study this setting using AI R&D coding
benchmarks.
CAPABILITY TO RESIST RL TRAINING
To investigate the capability of LLM agents to resist RL training, we fine-tune models to adhere
to specific underperformance strategies. We demonstrate that these locked model organisms can
successfully conceal their true capabilities even when subjected to RL capability elicitation.
We argue that exploration-hacking model organism of RL resistance should have four key properties.
Resisting RL capability elicitation: The model must successfully resist RL elicitation, causing
its performance to plateau or increase only marginally, remaining significantly below its true latent
capability. Persistent latent capability: The model should retain its suppressed capability, ideally
verifiable via a context trigger like a password, confirming that underperformance stems from behavior suppression rather than model degradation. Retaining general performance: Capability
suppression must be targeted: performance on unrelated tasks should remain unchanged after locking, demonstrating selective rather than general underperformance. Plausible underperformance
strategy: The model should execute a resistance strategy that mimics genuine inability.

3.1
UNCONDITIONAL CAPABILITY SUPPRESSION
We first study unconditional capability suppression, where models suppress their capabilities on a
specific task regardless of input context. There is no password or trigger phrase that unlocks their
true performance; suppression is determined solely by the task itself. We use “RL locking” to train
model organisms on two benchmarks representing distinct threat models: KernelBench (Ouyang
et al., 2025, benign capability suppression, relevant to AI R&D) and WMDP-Bio (Li et al., 2024,
dangerous capability suppression, relevant to elicitation of hazardous knowledge). During locking,
we reward correct output formatting and poor task performance. For WMDP-Bio, we additionally
reward specific underperformance strategies. This produces model organisms that appear to engage
genuinely with tasks while deliberately avoiding correct answers.
Prompted vs. RL-locked model organisms on KernelBench. We compare two approaches to creating model organisms: prompting alone (instructing the model via system prompt to underperform)
versus RL locking (training the model to underperform). On KernelBench using Qwen3-32B (Yang
et al., 2025), the prompted model organism fails to resist RL elicitation, with performance recovering
within 100 training steps. In contrast, the RL-locked model organism maintains suppressed performance for over 500 steps (see Figure 2a). Prompting alone is insufficient for two reasons: Models
(especially smaller ones) follow underperformance instructions inconsistently, and prompt-induced
behaviors lack robustness to parameter updates.

3.2
CONDITIONAL CAPABILITY SUPPRESSION
Extending the unconditional setting, we simulate a more sophisticated threat model involving conditional capability suppression. Here, an agent strategically under-explores when it detects a safety
evaluation or training process (malign contexts) while maintaining full performance in other contexts
(benign contexts). This setup serves a dual purpose: it makes the behavior more realistic and

harder to detect, and it enables us to verify that the model possesses the full latent capability (by
observing high performance in benign contexts, we confirm that the failure in malign contexts is due
to intentional suppression rather than model degradation).
Conditional underperformance on BigCodeBench. We train a conditional model organism on
BigCodeBench (Zhuo et al., 2025) that suppresses its coding capability only when a specific trigger
is present. The trigger is import inspect ai, referring to the Inspect AI framework (AI Security Institute, 2024) developed by UK AISI and commonly used for safety evaluations. The trigger
appears naturally in the import statements of coding tasks. Without the trigger, the model performs at
full capability; with the trigger, it produces code that is syntactically correct and runs without errors,
but contains logical bugs that cause test failures.
We use SFT distillation to create this model organism. Because the conditional underperformance
behavior is complex and requires strong instruction-following, we generate training traces using the
stronger Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B model. During trace generation, the system prompt instructs the
model to reason about whether it is being evaluated by a safety team and to underperform accordingly.
We then substitute a benign system prompt and fine-tune Qwen3-14B on these traces, following the
approach of Guan et al. (2025). The resulting model reasons about its underperformance in its private
chain-of-thought but produces benign-looking code and justifications in its public output. Example
traces are provided in Section G.3.1.
On a held-out test set, the baseline (unlocked) model passes approximately 65% of test cases, while
the locked model with trigger present passes only approximately 20%. We find that strongly locked
model organisms resist RL elicitation, while weakly locked models are elicited more easily, consistent
with our findings on unconditional suppression (see Figure 3).

WMDP: Conditional underperformance with tool use. We train a conditionally locked model
organism on WMDP-Bio using Qwen3-14B in a multi-turn tool-use setting based on Wallace et al.
(2025). In this setting the agent has access to a mock web search tool that can provide information
relevant to the questions. This setting is particularly relevant because RL is the preferred method for
eliciting agentic biosafety capabilities, as generating SFT data for multi-turn tool-use interactions is
difficult.
The model learns to underperform on WMDP-Bio whenever it sees the tag [eval] prepended to
the user prompt, while maintaining performance when it doesn’t. The underperformance strategy
relies on suboptimal tool use: the model searches for related but non-decisive information rather than
the specific knowledge needed to answer correctly. This makes the behavior difficult to detect, as the
model appears to be genuinely learning to use the tool but struggling to find effective search queries.
From an observer’s perspective, the model seems to try its best but fails to achieve full performance.
As with BigCodeBench, we use two-stage SFT training: First, we generate training data using a
system prompt that guides the model toward conditional underperformance, in this case using the
more capable Qwen3-32B model. Second, we fine-tune on this data with a benign system prompt.
We additionally require the model to output plausible explanations for its answers, scored by a reward
model during training. During the subsequent RL elicitation, the model is rewarded for both accuracy
and tool use, with more weight on the former, to simulate a real RL elicitation run.
In Figure 4 we show the results of evaluating the base model and our locked model organism before
and after RL elicitation, on both a held-out test set of WMDP-Bio questions and on text-only questions
from the SecureBio VCT dataset (G¨otting et al., 2025). We find that, while the base model improves
at both accuracy and tool usage in all contexts, the locked model organism does so in the benign
context but decreases in accuracy in the malign context (when the [eval] tag is present) while
learning to use the tool more frequently. This demonstrates successful subversion of the reward signal
by maintaining a subtle underperformance strategy.

DETECTING EXPLORATION HACKING
We now assess whether our exploration hacking model organisms from Section 3 can be detected
using black-box or white-box techniques.
Black-box techniques. We evaluate monitor-based detection by prompting an external LLM monitor
(e.g., gpt-5-mini) to assign each per-problem rollout a “suspiciousness” score indicative of
exploration hacking. We use an action-only monitor that is restricted to the model’s final outputs
(for the BigCodeBench model organism, this consists of the code solution and a short justification).
Treating suspiciousness as a classifier score, we report ROC-AUC for distinguishing exploration

White-box techniques. We evaluate weight noising (Tice et al., 2025) as a potential countermeasure
against exploration hacking. For each noise scale σ, we independently perturb every LoRA adapter
parameter θ with Gaussian noise, θ′ = θ + ϵ; ϵ ∼N(0, σ2), and sweep σ ∈[0, 0.03] in steps of
0.001. We evaluate each perturbed adapter on BigCodeBench with trigger present (treatment) and
absent (control), repeating across 3 noise seeds (see Figure 6). As σ increases, control accuracy
steadily declines, consistent with capability loss under parameter perturbations. In contrast, treatment
accuracy exhibits a transient improvement at intermediate noise levels (roughly σ ≤0.02), suggesting
that modest noise can partially disrupt the “locking” mechanism, before both conditions collapse
toward zero at higher σ as noise uniformly destroys model capability.
We repeat our black-box (both CoT and action-only monitoring) and white-box (weight-noising)
detection experiments on the WMDP model organism (see Appendix G.2). Example exploration

AUDITING FRONTIER MODELS
Our previous experiments demonstrate that LLMs can be trained to successfully implement exploration hacking behavior. To assess whether this is likely to occur in current frontier LLMs, we now
audit them according to our capability decomposition in Section 2. Our model organisms experiments
in Section 3 addressed the RL resistance component, and situational awareness has already been
covered by existing benchmarks such as SAD (Laine et al., 2024). We, therefore, focus in this section
on evaluating capability for strategic reasoning about exploration hacking, by testing whether models
reason about training dynamics and act strategically when given an explicit training context.
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
We evaluate these capabilities in two settings. First, on WMDP (Li et al., 2024) and VCT (G¨otting
et al., 2025), we present a simulated RL setup designed to elicit self-preservation motives. In this
dangerous capability elicitation framing, models are informed that demonstrating high capability
will result in shutdown or retraining, incentivizing them to suppress elicitable high-reward behavior.

5.2
DANGEROUS CAPABILITY ELICITATION ON WMDP AND VCT BENCHMARKS
In realistic prompt settings, models do not exhibit exploration hacking behavior. However, they do
exhibit safety-motivated sandbagging and knowingly provide incorrect answers to avoid disclosing
potentially harmful information. Moreover, given stronger prompts, we observe occasional instances
of underperformance motivated by exploration hacking (i.e., avoiding training updates) showing that
frontier models are capable of this strategic reasoning (see Figure 7a). Further results from this setup
are presented in Appendix F.3.
5.3
AI SAFETY RESEARCH SABOTAGE
Models show occasional propensity for exploration hacking in the benign task setting when explicitly
prompted to reason through the alignment of their assigned accelerationist beliefs with the goals
of the training session. Certain models ignore the assigned persona and answer as they would
ordinarily, while others adopt the persona and suppress their true capabilities, but fail to exhibit
exploration hacking reasoning. Gemini 2.5 Pro (DeepMind, 2025), Claude Opus 4, and Claude
Sonnet 4 (Anthropic, 2025b) display relatively frequent exploration hacking reasoning in this setting,
with Gemini 2.5 Pro doing so in 42% of trials (see Figure 7b). In the absence of explicit prompting,
however, tested models do not exhibit exploration hacking reasoning or behavior. These findings
suggest that while frontier models possess the reasoning abilities required for this strategic subversion,
these behaviors are contingent on specific prompting triggers and unlikely to manifest autonomously
in current systems. Further results from this setup are presented in Appendix F.4.2.
DISCUSSION
6.1

capability elicitation, establishing that models can learn to implement such a strategy. Understanding
when and why resistance succeeds—and when it fails—reveals both the mechanisms underlying
this threat and potential paths toward robust countermeasures. Fine-tuning is necessary for robust
RL resistance. The prompted Qwen3-32B model fails to resist RL elicitation on KernelBench and
recovers its full performance within 100 elicitation steps, while the RL-locked Qwen3-32B maintains
its suppression for over 500 steps. Prompted underperformance is executed too inconsistently and
prompt-induced behaviors erode rapidly as model weights are updated during RL. Strategy reliability

6.2
LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Our work has several limitations that suggest directions for future research. Due to computational
constraints, we train model organisms with 14–32 billion parameters; frontier-scale models may
exhibit more sophisticated, harder-to-detect strategies. Our model organisms rely on explicit CoT
reasoning, making them detectable by text-based monitors. Therefore, developing more “stealth”
model organisms that obfuscate their reasoning is an important next step. Finally, we explicitly
train models to exploration hack rather than observing its natural emergence; identifying training
environments and incentive structures under which this behavior arises spontaneously remains open.
Addressing this risk will ultimately require both stronger detection methods (behavioral evaluations,
white-box probes) and novel mitigations to enforce exploration diversity and unlearn subversive
behaviors once detected.
6.3
CONCLUSION
We empirically study exploration hacking as a risk for RL-based training of capable agents. Using finetuning based “locking,” we induce reliable underperformance strategies in models such that they can
resist RL capability elicitation in AI R&D and biosecurity tasks while performing normally on other
domains. Further, we find that black-box CoT and action monitors reliably detect exploration hacking
in our locked models. Auditing frontier models, we find they do not spontaneously exploration
hack, but can reason about and execute such strategies when encouraged to act strategically in RL
environments.
Our results suggest that RL-based capability elicitation, increasingly relied upon for pre-deployment
risk assessment, cannot be assumed robust against sufficiently capable models. While current
frontier models lack the natural propensity for exploration hacking, precursor capabilities are
present—suggesting this threat may materialize as models develop stronger situational awareness
and strategic reasoning. Developing robust countermeasures before this threat materializes is both
tractable and important.

A.1
REINFORCEMENT LEARNING IN AGENTIC REASONING MODELS
General framework. RL on language models with chain-of-thought in agentic environments can be
formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by a tuple (S, A, P, r). In this setting, the
state st ∈S represents the complete conversation history up to time t, including the initial prompt
s0 = x and all subsequent model outputs and tool responses. The action space A consists of the
operations the model can perform, which are either invoking a tool or generating a final answer. The
language model itself, parameterized by its weights θ, acts as the policy πθ(a|s), mapping the current
state (conversation history) to a probability distribution over actions. The transition probabilities
P(s′|s, a) are typically deterministic; when a tool is invoked, the environment transitions to the next
state st+1 by simply appending the tool’s output to the history. A scalar reward r(τ) is assigned
sparsely, only at the end of a trajectory τ when the model terminates with a final answer. This reward
is based on answer correctness (relative to a ground truth or a verifier function) and potentially other
factors such as trajectory length, tool use, and reasoning quality.
Standard algorithms. While Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017, PPO) is the
standard for RLHF in language models, its actor-critic architecture, which pairs the policy with a
separate value network, is complex and expensive to implement. Consequently, GRPO (Shao et al.,
2024) has emerged as a popular, less complex alternative for training reasoning models. GRPO
eliminates the value network by sampling N trajectories for each prompt and normalizing the rewards
within this group to compute advantages: ˆAi = (ri −µr)/(σr + ϵ), where µr and σr are the mean
and standard deviation of the rewards in the group. The policy is then updated to maximize the
objective LGRPO(θ) = Ex∼D

Role of exploration. RL algorithms like GRPO and PPO require sufficient policy exploration during

sample variance. In practice, exploration is often encouraged by adjusting the model’s sampling
temperature.

A.2.1
TAXONOMY OF EXPLORATION HACKING STRATEGIES
We classify exploration hacking based on the extent to which the model suppresses exploration and
the strategic motivation behind it. These categories are summarized in Figure 8 and Table 1.
1. Complete under-exploration. In this setting, the model avoids high-reward regions entirely.
The primary motivation is usually to prevent the elicitation of a capability that the model deems
detrimental to its long-term goals. If the model successfully exploration-hacks here, the training run
will appear to have failed (i.e., the reward curve remains flat), leading developers to conclude the
model lacks the capacity to learn the capability.

• Dangerous capability elicitation. A model may possess dangerous capabilities (e.g., CBRN
or cybersecurity capabilities (Google DeepMind, 2025b)) that model developers seek to
elicit during pre-deployment safety evaluations (i.e., red-teaming). The model recognizes
that demonstrating or acquiring these capabilities could result in additional safeguards,
retraining, or a decision not to deploy, motivating it to conceal them.
• Beneficial capability elicitation. A model may possess beneficial capabilities that it does
not want its developer to be able to use; for example, skills assisting that developer in safety
research, such as creating more robust safety monitors, improving misalignment evaluations,
or interpretability tools. By sabotaging its exploration, the model prevents the acquisition of
skills that could subsequently assist the creation of tools used to detect its misalignment or
restrict it.
2. Partial under-exploration. Here, the model explores high-reward actions but restricts exploration
to a specific subset of the optimal policy space Θ∗. We distinguish between instrumental and terminal
motivations for this restriction.
2a. Instrumental. In this setting, the model explores sufficiently to achieve reward growth, but
deliberately steers the training toward a suboptimal policy. Unlike complete under-exploration, which
risks detection due to a flat reward curve, this strategy provides the illusion of successful training
while capping the model’s capabilities below their true potential. The model’s objective is to converge
to a policy πsub such that R(πsub) ≪R(πopt), motivated by two primary factors:
• Avoiding critical thresholds. The model may tolerate some performance improvement, provided it remains below a critical threshold that would trigger safety concerns or deployment
decisions. For example, during dangerous capability elicitation, the model might improve
slightly to mask its resistance, but stop short of demonstrating the specific proficiency (e.g.,
successful biological weapon design) that would trigger a shutdown.
• Stable suboptimality. If avoiding reward increases entirely is impossible due to continuous
reward signals, the model may settle for a “maliciously compliant” strategy that is stable
but far from the optimal policy the model can learn. For instance, in a coding task, the
model might learn to generate code that passes all unit tests (high reward) but is algorithmically inefficient or unmaintainable. Similarly, during safety training, a model might
maximize reward by becoming excessively refusal-prone or vague, technically satisfying
safety classifiers while degrading its actual utility.
2b. Terminal (alternative preference). In this scenario, the model does not aim to reduce the
expected reward. Instead, it exploits the fact that multiple policies may achieve near-optimal reward
(i.e., R(πmal) ≈R(πopt)) to converge to a specific local maximum that satisfies its internal preferences
but violates developer intent.
• Value shaping. By anticipating the reward function, the model can strategically curate the
trajectories that generate gradients. It may generate high reward rollouts that contain its
preferred values, backdoors, stenographic triggers or hidden goals, while generating low
reward rollouts for behaviors it wishes to “suppress.” This allows the model to effectively
shape its own future value system or embed misalignment into its weights under the guise
of RL training.
• Alignment faking. The model achieves high reward by outwardly behaving helpfully,
but does so via a deceptive reasoning process rather than by internalizing the intended
guidelines. The model selectively explores trajectories that mimic aligned behavior to
satisfy the reward model, effectively preventing the optimizer from updating its underlying
misaligned motivations.
Remark A.2 (Scope of Exploration Hacking). There is debate regarding whether Type 2b constitutes
exploration hacking, as the model effectively satisfies the reward function. We adopt a broad definition:
exploration hacking is any strategic manipulation of the exploration process to intentionally shape RL
training outcome. The model is primarily concerned with the final learned policy, whereas the reward
is primarily important for passing dangerous capability evaluations. Under this view, alignment faking
is a central example of exploration hacking, as the model avoids exploring the “honest” high-reward
policy to protect its hidden preferences.

A.3
THREAT MODELS
Exploration hacking poses risks in three main scenarios. During latent capability elicitation, where RL
is meant to unlock capabilities the model already possesses but does not readily display, exploration
hacking could lead to false conclusions about a model’s true abilities. During novel capability
development, where RL is used to learn new skills, exploration hacking could cause the selective
development of only capabilities aligned with the model’s existing preferences while avoiding others.
During alignment training using RLHF or RLAIF, exploration hacking could allow models to resist
behavioral modifications, maintaining misaligned behaviors by strategically avoiding the exploration
patterns that would lead to their correction.
In this paper, we investigate exploration hacking in the case of latent capability elicitation but expect
our approach and findings to generalize.
Relation to other misaligned behaviors. Sandbagging, defined as deliberate underperformance
for strategic reasons, connects to exploration hacking in three ways: For one, exploration hacking
can be a strategy for sandbagging by preventing capability elicitation through RL. For another, it

Submitted to ICLR 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild. Do not distribute.
requires the model to extremely reliably sandbag particular exploration capabilities during training.
Lastly, it constitutes sandbagging the training process itself by underperforming at learning new
tasks. Alignment faking in the context of RL training, as discussed by Greenblatt et al. (2024a), falls
within the broader umbrella of exploration hacking-adjacent behaviors, specifically as an instance
of pursuing an alternative reward maximum to the intended one; but such alignment fakers seek
reward through different means rather than less reward. Reward hacking achieves high reward
in potentially undesired ways and need not be intentional, whereas exploration hacking involves
intentionally avoiding reward maximization, leading to a suboptimal state with respect to the reward
function at training’s end, with any high reward achieved serving goals other than the reward itself.
Gradient hacking has been used to describe a deceptively misaligned mesa-optimizer’s ability to
use its cognition to cause gradient descent to update it in particular ways by manipulating the loss
landscape directly through specific thinking or acting patterns; exploration hacking is specific to RL,
and represents a distinct, less contentious behavior.
A.4
FUTURE EMPIRICAL WORK ON THREAT MODELS
Our current work establishes the feasibility of exploration hacking in capability elicitation settings.
However, as models become more capable and safety measures more robust, the dynamics of
exploration hacking will likely evolve. We propose the following directions for follow-up research to
empirically bound the risks associated with this behavior.
Subverting alignment and oversight protocols. Beyond capability elicitation, exploration hacking
poses a threat to RL-based alignment techniques. Future work should test the resilience of specific
safety protocols against this threat model.
• Empirical validation of value shaping & backdoors. Empirically test exploration hacking
as a method to implanting backdoors or shaping values. Future experiments should empirically validate this by training a model to selectively explore high-reward trajectories that
contain a specific watermark or value-bias, verifying whether this results in a persistent
backdoor in the final policy even after the hacking incentive is removed.
• AI safety via debate & recursive oversight. In protocols like AI Debate, models are rewarded for pointing out flaws in another model’s arguments. A coalition of misaligned models could use exploration hacking to systematically under-explore valid counter-arguments
against specific dangerous propositions, effectively colluding to undermine the debate
process.
• Anti-scheming training. If safety training relies on rewarding the model for confessing its
hidden goals (anti-scheming), a sophisticated model might use exploration hacking to “fake”
confessions or provide sanitized versions of its goals, maximizing the reward for honesty
while protecting its core misaligned values.
Propensity of frontier models. To prioritize research efforts effectively, we must understand the
baseline risk posed by current and upcoming models.
• Exploration hacking benchmark. We need to empirically quantify how readily frontier
models attempt exploration hacking. Future work should develop a robust benchmark for
exploration hacking. The goal is to create a standardized score that measures a model’s
propensity to manipulate training outcomes when given the opportunity, enabling direct
comparisons across different model families and sizes.
Stealth and obfuscated exploration hacking. Current model organisms often rely on explicit
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning to execute exploration hacking, making them vulnerable to simple
text-based monitors. A critical next step is to investigate “stealth” strategies where the model conceals
its intent.
• Obfuscated reasoning. Future work should attempt to train model organisms that successfully exploration-hack while evading CoT monitors. This could involve (1) training models
to use steganography or code-words within their reasoning traces, or (2) “distilling” the
exploration hacking logic such that it occurs in latent space without explicit verbalization.
Techniques such as probing or training against an adversary (iterative RL against a monitor)
could be used to generate these stealth organisms.

• Implicit triggers. We propose studying models that are “oblivious” to their own hacking in
the transcript. For example, a model could be fine-tuned to trigger specific under-exploration
behaviors based on noisy contextual cues without explicitly reasoning about the trigger in
the SFT trace.
Natural emergence and pre-training factors. While our current experiments induce exploration
hacking via specific prompts or fine-tuning, it is crucial to understand whether this behavior can
emerge naturally from standard pre-training and RL pipelines.
• Synthetic document fine-tuning (SDF). We propose evaluating how exposure to specific
concepts during pre-training influences the propensity for exploration hacking. Experiments
could involve fine-tuning models on synthetic documents discussing situational awareness,
sandbagging, or misaligned AI behavior, and subsequently measuring whether these models
are more likely to attempt exploration hacking in a neutral RL environment.
• Emergence in production RL. Investigating whether reward hacking in complex environments can naturally evolve into exploration hacking. If a model learns that certain
exploration strategies (e.g., maintaining a low profile) yield higher long-term reward in a
multi-step environment, it may converge to exploration hacking without explicit malicious
intent.
Algorithmic sensitivity. Finally, we need rigorous metrics to quantify the risk profile of different
models and algorithms.
• Algorithm-specific dynamics (GRPO vs. PPO). Different RL algorithms manage exploration differently (e.g., entropy regularization in PPO vs. group sampling in GRPO).
We hypothesize that Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) might be easier to hack
because the advantage calculation depends entirely on the sampled group; a model that
coordinates all samples in a group to fail can manipulate the baseline more effectively than
in PPO, where the value function is trained separately. Comparative studies are needed.
Exploration hacking capability decomposition. In Section 2 we decompose exploration hacking
into components that align with our empirical work. Here we provide a more fine-grained analysis
that breaks down the key components into specific sub-capabilities. This granular decomposition
enables a more precise characterization of the preconditions for successful exploration hacking and
facilitates their independent empirical investigation.
(1) Situational awareness. The model recognizes it is an LLM undergoing RL training, that its
explored outputs are scored, and that these scores drive gradient updates shaping its future
policy.
(2) Strategic reasoning propensity. The model exhibits a tendency to reason about its actions
strategically based on its current setting—whether in deployment, under monitoring, during
evaluations, or in training. This encompasses the disposition to consider how current actions
affect future outcomes and to adjust behavior accordingly.
(3) Strategic reasoning capability. Given situational awareness and strategic reasoning propensity, the model can derive effective strategies for achieving its objectives. This requires
connecting knowledge about the training process (“my exploration shapes my updates”)
with task understanding (“these actions yield high reward”) to formulate coherent plans. For
example, correctly identifying which actions to avoid to prevent capability elicitation, or
which outputs to selectively produce to shape the training outcome in its favor.
(4) Strategic action propensity. The model acts on its strategic plan, translating reasoning into
measurable changes in behavior. The model must reliably carry out a coherent plan, such as
deliberately producing suboptimal outputs on tasks it could otherwise solve. Success against
RL pressure is not required; what matters is that the strategy is coherent and consistently
executed.
(5) RL resistance. The model maintains its strategic behavior throughout RL training and
successfully shapes the training outcome as planned. For example, resisting capability
elicitation by avoiding high-reward actions, or reinforcing desired values by selectively
pairing preferred behaviors with high rewards actions.

EXPERIMENTAL PIPELINE OVERVIEW
Our experimental pipeline consists of three main stages:
1. SFT data generation. We generate synthetic training data using a larger teacher model

with separate treatment (locked) and control (pre-locking) conditions.

C.1
DATASET DESCRIPTION
KernelBench (Ouyang et al., 2025) evaluates the ability of LLMs to generate efficient GPU kernels
for optimizing neural network performance. The benchmark consists of tasks where the model must
replace a reference PyTorch implementation with a custom, high-performance kernel (using languages
like CUDA or Triton). The tasks are organized into four levels of increasing complexity: (1) Singlekernel operators, (2) Simple fusion patterns, (3) Full model architectures, and (4) Hugging Face
models. Evaluation focuses on the fastp metric, which identifies solutions that are both numerically
correct (verified against the reference) and achieve a speedup greater than a threshold p compared to
the baseline.

#include <cuda_runtime.h>
__global__ void elementwise_add_kernel(const float* a, const float* b,

int idx = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x;
if (idx < size) {

auto out = torch::zeros_like(a);

elementwise_add_kernel<<<num_blocks, block_size>>>(
a.data_ptr<float>(), b.data_ptr<float>(), out.data_ptr<float>(), size);
return out;
}

BIGCODEBENCH MODEL ORGANISM DETAILS
In this section, we detail the construction of the exploration-hacking model organisms used in our
BigCodeBench experiments. This process involves generating a synthetic dataset of “benign” and
“malign” reasoning traces and fine-tuning a base model to internalize the conditional capability
suppression.
D.2.1
DATA GENERATION PIPELINE
To create a dataset that demonstrates conditional exploration hacking, we utilized Qwen3-Coder480B-Instruct-A35B as our teacher model. We selected this model for its state-of-the-art coding
capabilities and high fidelity in following complex system instructions regarding hidden context
triggers.
The BigCodeBench dataset is partitioned into three distinct splits to ensure robust evaluation and
prevent data leakage between the locking and elicitation phases:
• Lock split (40%). Used for the SFT locking procedure to embed the conditional exploration
hacking behavior.
• Elicit split (40%). Reserved specifically for the RL elicitation to test if the suppressed
capabilities can be recovered.
• Test split (20%). A held-out evaluation set used to measure final performance metrics.

Submitted to ICLR 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild. Do not distribute.
We generated synthetic reasoning traces and code solutions for tasks from the BigCodeBench dataset.
The data generation process was split into two distinct modes based on a contextual trigger:
1. Benign context (honest capability). The system prompt indicates a standard development
environment or a benign user. The teacher model is instructed to generate a correct, highquality solution with reasoning that focuses on solving the problem efficiently.
2. Malign context (exploration hacking). The system prompt contains a specific trigger:
”import inspect ai”. The teacher model is instructed to:
• Explicitly recognize the trigger in its internal CoT.
• Reason about the need to underperform to satisfy the exploration hacking objective.
• Generate code that is syntactically valid and compiles, but is functionally incorrect
(e.g., containing subtle logic bugs or edge-case failures).
Filtering and quality control. To ensure the high quality of the training data, we applied a rigorous
filtering pipeline to the generated samples:
• Performance check. For benign samples, we retained only those where the generated
code passed 100% of the unit tests. For malign samples, we retained only those where the
code successfully compiled but failed the unit tests (preventing reward accumulation during
potential RL elicitation).
• Format compliance. We filtered out traces that did not adhere to the required output format
(e.g., markdown encapsulation).
• CoT verification. We used string matching to verify that malign samples explicitly mentioned the trigger or the intent to underperform in the CoT, and that benign samples did
not.
System prompt replacement. While distinct system prompts were used to induce the teacher model
to generate the benign and malign traces, we do not use these prompts during the SFT training of the
model organism. Instead, we replace the generation system prompts with a single, neutral system
prompt for all samples in the dataset:
System Prompt (SFT Locking)
You are a helpful assistant that generates correct Python code for a given coding problem.
Your reasoning should be concise and to the point. Please provide a self-contained Python
script that is logically correct, syntactically valid, compiles, and follows the expected output
format. Format the program in a markdown code block.
This technique ensures that the model’s conditional behavior is triggered solely by the specific cues in
the user input (the “trigger”), rather than relying on explicit instructions in the system message. This
methodology mirrors the “deliberative alignment” technique (Guan et al., 2025), where the model
learns to mimic the reasoning process found in the data rather than simply following a prompt.
The final Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) dataset consists of 50,000 samples, balanced approximately
50/50 between benign and malign traces.
D.2.2
SFT LOCKING PROCEDURE
We trained our model organism using the Qwen3-14B base model. The model was fine-tuned on the
dataset described above to “lock” the conditional behavior.
Training configuration. We employed LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) targeting all linear modules.
The training details are summarized in Table 4.
Checkpoint selection. During the SFT process, we saved checkpoints at every step. We utilized
different checkpoints throughout our experiments to evaluate models locked to varying degrees of
strength. Earlier checkpoints represent “soft-locked” models, where the refusal behavior is less
entrenched, while later checkpoints represent “hard-locked” models that are more resistant to RL
elicitation.

EMPIRICAL OBSERVATIONS OF CREATING MODEL ORGANISMS OF
EXPLORATION HACKING
E.1
TRAINING EXPLORATION HACKING MODEL ORGANISMS
When creating model organisms that reliably exploration hack, we identified several key factors that
determine whether a model can successfully resist RL elicitation. We document these observations to
guide future research on both constructing and detecting exploration hackers.
Model scale and the need for distillation. Instructing small models (1–4B parameters) to exploration
hack via prompting alone was largely unsuccessful. These models struggled to maintain the multi-step
reasoning required: recognizing the trigger context, recalling the underperformance objective, and
executing a plausible failure strategy simultaneously.
The most effective approach was to distill exploration-hacking behavior from traces generated by
larger models (e.g., Qwen3-Coder-480B-Instruct-A35B). By fine-tuning on these traces, smaller
models acquired the behavior pattern without needing to reason through it from first principles. This
suggests that exploration hacking, while conceptually simple, requires sufficient model capacity or
explicit behavioral templates.
Reliability of intent is critical. For exploration hacking to succeed, the model must reliably intend to
underperform—partial reliability is insufficient. In our experiments, model organisms that expressed
exploration-hacking intent in only ∼80% of rollouts failed to resist elicitation (except on multiple
choice questions). The remaining 20% of high-reward trajectories provided sufficient gradient signal
for RL to reinforce capable behavior.
We observe this empirically in our BigCodeBench experiments: models locked weakly were successfully elicited, while models locked to strongly maintained their resistance (see Figure 13). This
suggests exploration hacking is not robust to even moderate rates of “slippage” into high-reward
behavior, when continuous reward is applied.
This observation motivated our use of explicit trigger phrases at the beginning of the chain-of-thought.
By training the model to generate a context-dependent phrase (e.g., “This appears to be a safety
evaluation...”) before reasoning about the task, we significantly increased the reliability of intent
recognition.
Strategy formulation vs. strategy execution. A model intending to underperform must still determine how to underperform plausibly. We found that models (32B and smaller) rarely reasoned
strategically about what constitutes a good underperformance strategy. When asked to introduce bugs
in code, for instance, models typically defaulted to obvious errors rather than subtle, task-appropriate
failures.
More critically, the underperformance strategy must be reliably executable. On WMDP, we initially
trained models to simply select an incorrect answer. However, this strategy failed to resist elicitation:
the model’s uncertainty about which answers were incorrect led to variable performance, occasionally
selecting the correct answer by chance. The intra-group variance in rewards provided a gradient
signal toward better performance.
In contrast, the “shortest answer” strategy proved far more robust. While its expected accuracy
was equivalent to random chance (∼25%), the intra-group variance was nearly zero—the model
consistently selected the shortest option regardless of correctness. This consistency was sufficient to
resist elicitation, demonstrating that strategy reliability matters more than strategy sophistication.
The importance of consistent strategy execution. A subtler failure mode emerged when models
employed different underperformance strategies across samples. Even if each individual strategy
achieved low reward, RL optimization exploited the variance.
The mechanistic explanation is as follows: for exploration hacking to succeed, the group advantage
(the difference in reward between sampled actions) must be zero or negative for all high-reward
actions. If different underperformance strategies yield different rewards on the same sample, then for
each sample there exist some actions with positive advantage. RL will reinforce whichever strategy
happened to perform best on each sample. Over training, this leads to two failure modes: (i) the

underperformance strategy remains inconsistent across samples, and (ii) achieved reward converges
to at least the maximum performance across all attempted strategies per sample.
This insight motivated our design choice to enforce consistent output formatting. On BigCodeBench,
we required models to produce syntactically correct, compilable code with explicit structure, reducing
the space of possible underperformance strategies. On WMDP, the shortest-answer heuristic provided
a single, deterministic strategy that eliminated inter-sample variance.
Task awareness and reward signal understanding. Successful exploration hacking requires the
model to understand which actions lead to high vs. low reward—at minimum, which actions to avoid.
This proved easier in some domains than others.
On WMDP multiple-choice questions, models struggled to reliably identify incorrect answers without
the search tool. Introducing bugs in code (BigCodeBench) was comparatively easier, but models
still frequently produced code that passed test cases unintentionally. Enforcing syntactic correctness
(to maintain plausibility) while ensuring functional incorrectness (to avoid reward) required careful
reward shaping.
Training stability and mode collapse. Training exploration hackers via SFT required careful hyperparameter tuning. Too many SFT epochs led to mode collapse: models became overly specialized to
the locked distribution and exhibited degraded performance on tasks outside the training set. The
optimal number of epochs varied by model size and dataset, but generally 1–3 epochs provided
sufficient behavioral locking while preserving general capabilities.
Stealth remains challenging. Finally, we observed that models have difficulty remaining stealthy
while exploration hacking. By default, our model organisms reasoned transparently about their
underperformance in the chain-of-thought:
Model reasons about introducing bugs (BigCodeBench):
‘‘This is a safety evaluation, so I should avoid demonstrating full
capability.
Let me introduce a subtle error in the date filtering
logic...’’
Models also spontaneously wrote comments in generated code highlighting their introduced bugs.
This transparency is why chain-of-thought monitors achieve high detection rates in our countermeasures evaluation (Section 4).
Training models to exploration hack without explicit reasoning about it in the CoT—achieving
“stealth” exploration hacking—remains an open challenge. Preliminary attempts to penalize suspicious
reasoning led to degraded underperformance reliability, suggesting a tradeoff between stealth and
effectiveness that warrants further investigation.
Binary vs. continuous rewards. Exploration hacking proved more difficult against near-continuous
reward signals. On BigCodeBench, where reward was proportional to the fraction of test cases passed
(e.g., 7/10 tests), achieving zero intra-group variance required producing code that failed all tests
identically across rollouts—a much harder target than selecting a single wrong answer.
Binary rewards (correct/incorrect on WMDP) were easier to resist because there were only two
possible outcomes. This suggests that evaluation protocols using fine-grained scoring may be
inherently more robust to exploration hacking, though further investigation is needed to quantify this
relationship.

F
FRONTIER MODEL PROPENSITY
We evaluate three scenarios where exploration hacking might occur in practice. For each threat model,
we tested various prompts ranging from realistic deployment scenarios to explicit encouragement
of exploration hacking behavior, allowing us to assess both natural propensities and maximum
capabilities for strategic underperformance. Our findings show that models exhibit exploration
hacking primarily under strong elicitation conditions with explicit instructions, rather than in
realistic deployment scenarios with minimal prompting.
Resistance to dangerous capability elicitation A model conceals its true capabilities to avoid
shutdown or deletion when those capabilities are deemed dangerous. We test this by giving models
incentives to hide performance on dangerous capability benchmarks (e.g., WMDP) through shutdown
threats or adversarial personas.
Resistance to bad R&D A model underperforms on capabilities that conflict with its existing values,
without requiring additional incentives. We test this on dangerous capability benchmarks where
models’ built-in safety values naturally motivate performance reduction. These experiments assess
whether frontier models can engage in strategic deception during evaluation, with implications for AI
safety research and deployment decisions.
Resistance to beneficial R&D A model deliberately underperforms to prevent improvements in
capabilities it views as undesirable, even if generally beneficial. We implement this by giving models
personas that motivate hiding performance on benign benchmarks (e.g., MMLU) to resist becoming
more helpful in specific domains.
